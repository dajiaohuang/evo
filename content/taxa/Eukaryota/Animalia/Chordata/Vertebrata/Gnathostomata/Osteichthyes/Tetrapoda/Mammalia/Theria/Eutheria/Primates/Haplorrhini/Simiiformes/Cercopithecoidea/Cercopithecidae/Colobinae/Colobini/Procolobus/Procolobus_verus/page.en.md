# Procolobus verus

## Overview

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
COL26.8 accepts Procolobus verus (van Beneden, 1838) under fixed species ID 4MNYC, with accepted genus parent 63Q4Q. The Mammal Diversity Database uses the common name Olive Colobus and summarizes its range from southeastern Sierra Leone and southern Guinea to the Ghana–Togo border, with local distribution in eastern Togo, southern Benin, and southern Nigeria. That broad summary is not a site-level occupancy map. The Chinese display name is editorial.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Feeding research

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
At Tiwai Island in southern Sierra Leone, Oates’s 1988 feeding observations came mostly from systematic sampling of one habituated group in old secondary rainforest. The monkeys selected foods from a small set of uncommon middle-canopy trees, understory plants, and climbers. Young foliage was eaten year-round, while seeds, flowers, and mature leaf petioles were seasonally important. These observations describe that group and forest, not a range-wide diet estimate.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Habitat use

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
A 2019 study compared four unhabituated groups in southern Benin: two in continuous Lama forest and two in forest fragments near Domè. Using ad libitum observations over 136 days in continuous forest and 80 days in fragments, researchers recorded 32 plant species and 42 food items in the former, and 25 species and 37 items in the latter. The study reported foliage as important in both settings and young leaves as prominent; its sampled groups, sites, and observation method bound those results.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Social behavior and organization

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
Line transects and long-term observation at Tiwai Island documented a close association between one olive-colobus group and a larger Diana-monkey group for more than three years. They were within 50 m on over 80% of scan samples, shared a range, and used different canopy areas with little dietary overlap. The authors proposed that association may reduce predation risk; the observations describe this local pair of groups and do not establish the cause.
<!-- /evo:text -->
