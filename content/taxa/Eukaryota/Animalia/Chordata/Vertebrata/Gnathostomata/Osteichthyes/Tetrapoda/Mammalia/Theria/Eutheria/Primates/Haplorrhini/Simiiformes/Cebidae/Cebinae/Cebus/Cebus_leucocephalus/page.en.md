# Cebus leucocephalus


## A traceable leucocephalus-labelled sample

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
Row 146 on page 12 of Supplementary Table S1 labels USNM 443629 as C. a. leucocephalus, from Nulita, Apure, VEN, with mitochondrial cytochrome-b accession JN409293 and control-region accession JQ317642. It enters the two-marker DS2, while its DS1 column is unmarked. This traces the study label, voucher, locality and sequences without independently examining or redetermining the specimen. One Venezuelan sample is neither the species’ complete range nor evidence of current occupancy in Colombia.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## The authors’ interpretation of clustering with adustus

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
The main text groups row 146 with rows 142 and 143, historically labelled C. a. adustus. Those samples are USNM 443501 and 443503, both recorded from El Rosario, Zulia, VEN. The authors interpret these forms as one subspecies while acknowledging unresolved relationships and polytomies in the genus. Mitochondrial affinity and the authors’ subspecies interpretation are stated separately, without becoming a confirmed synonymy in this catalogue. Clustering does not establish identical morphology, mating patterns or ecology.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## What two mitochondrial markers can address

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
Martins-Junior and colleagues (2018) analyze mitochondrial cytochrome b and the control region. Across the full study, new material comes from 72 capuchins, yielding 137 new sequences, alongside previously published sequences. These study-wide counts are not individual counts for this page’s species. Both markers belong to the mitochondrial genome, rather than two independent nuclear-genome lines of evidence. Clustering can trace historical names without measuring feeding, reproduction or population size, or independently establishing complete species boundaries.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Differences in author year and taxonomic rank

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
COL26.8 accepts Cebus leucocephalus Gray, 1865. MDD v2.5 lists C. leucocephalus Gray, 1866 as a synonym of C. albifrons and gives its restricted type locality as Santander, Colombia. This page preserves the difference in authorship year and rank rather than presenting one treatment as uncontested.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Historical genetic groups and local evidence gaps

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
An older COII study of 118 white-fronted capuchins recognized a leucocephalus group, but could not resolve relationships among the main genetic groups; the samples and names belonged to a broader C. albifrons research framework. A 2025 Colombian assessment says local biological and population information for the species is lacking. This evidence does not support turning the older name-group result into a natural history of an independently delimited species.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Modeled areas do not confirm occupancy

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
The 2020 Colombian atlas gives modeled surfaces of 38,085 km² for potential range and 11,597 km² for remnant range. A 2025 national assessment reports no GBIF species records and very little biological or population information. Range models and occurrence records answer different questions; the former are not confirmation of current occupancy.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Evidence limits

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/limitations/en -->
This page covers name disagreement, historical genetic groups, modeled distribution and the 2019 handbook account. The handbook name and ecological records have not been independently linked to a stable pinned COL concept. Attributable field ecology, complete range and population information remain insufficient, so the first-round gap stays open. Size fields lack underlying sample details; map models do not confirm occupancy. There is no external expert review.
New 2018 sample-tracing text uses the read molecular sampling, tree methods, Cebus results and discussion passages of the published SciELO article, figure 4 caption, and Supplementary Table S1 pages 10, 12 and 13; layouts on pages 10 and 12 were visually examined. Other table pages were not fully reviewed row by row. Figure 4 pixels, other supplements, raw GenBank sequences and museum specimens were not independently examined, and analyses were not rerun. The 2012 article was not obtained on this pass, so no fresh review of it is claimed. Historical sample names, sequence associations, type diagnoses and current COL identities remain distinct. These links do not complete independent field ecology or species delimitation; first-round primate gaps remain open, with no external expert review.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Appearance in the 2019 handbook

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/text/en -->
The 2019 EAZA account describes cinnamon-brown pelage, paler flanks and reddish-brown wrists, ankles and upper surfaces of the hands and feet. The tail becomes paler toward its tip and underneath. This describes the name as used in that edition; color alone cannot resolve the disputed species identity. Its male body-size field is 37–47.7 cm and tail length 39.2–49.9 cm; female measurements and mass are lacking. Sample size, age composition and measurement methods are unspecified, so these are reported fields rather than typical size or complete variation.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Habitat summary and missing observations

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/7/text/en -->
The same account lists lowland moist forest, semi-deciduous dry forest and mangroves without habitat-use proportions or dated species-specific transects. Reproduction, diet and behavior are all marked as lacking data. No feeding, group-composition or daily-activity account is inferred from other capuchins.
