# Pterodaustro guinazui


## Filamentous teeth form a close filtering structure

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
The long skull curves upward, with filamentous lower teeth differing from short upper teeth with spatulate crowns. The 2023 study records dentine, pulp cavities and thin enamel, supporting their identity as true teeth. Closely packed roots share a groove; the authors interpret this arrangement as helping retain food particles during jaw closure. Morphology and histology support filtering, while this study neither measures filtering efficiency nor identifies actual food items.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Eleven sections come from one jaw specimen

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
MIC-V55 is a partial skeleton from laminated shales of the Lagarcito Formation at Loma del Pterodaustro, San Luis, Argentina. Four transverse and seven coronal sections were made from one block containing left and right lower jaws and teeth, then examined with a polarizing microscope. Eleven sections provide different views of one specimen; they do not represent eleven animals or cover every age and the upper dentition.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Groove geometry and tooth attachment require separate evidence

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
No bony partitions separate neighboring roots in the filamentous region, supporting the authors’ aulacodont, or groove-set, interpretation. Their introduction separately notes individual sockets for the first seven thicker lower teeth, so the sampled region does not define the entire dentition. Typical cementum, Sharpey’s fibres and ligament attachment traces are absent. Tight root-to-wall contact could reflect fossil compression, leaving fusion of teeth to jaw bone unresolved.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Missing replacement teeth leave lifetime tooth generations unresolved

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
The MIC-V55 sections lack replacement teeth; occasional overlaps are interpreted as breakage or displacement. The authors argue against repeated replacement of the filamentous teeth but cannot distinguish one lifetime generation from two. No von Ebner growth lines were identified in dentine, with preservation and formation patterns still possible explanations. Tooth formation and replacement rates therefore remain unavailable, and this material does not resolve every tooth or developmental stage.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Growth evidence combines several long bones

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
The 2008 study samples humeri, femora, tibiae and other bones across differently sized individuals and compares bones within skeletons. Relative size uses each element’s length against the largest known corresponding element, rather than measured body mass. Femora, tibiae and radii better preserve growth records; early humeral remodeling limits age inference. Fast and slow deposition zones and arrested-growth lines require attention to bone identity, section position and preservation.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Bone tissue supports phased growth and eventual cessation

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
The 2008 authors interpret young individuals’ fibrolamellar bone as rapid growth, with closer growth marks and changes at bone ends supporting later slowing and cessation. Annual cycles are assumed by comparison with other reptiles. They propose sexual maturity at about two years and approximately 53% of the largest reference bone length, followed by several more years of growth. Maturity is inferred from changing deposition, not observed reproduction. Later-growth durations differ between abstract and discussion, so the curve does not establish a precisely measured lifespan.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Near-hatching skeletons make flight questions quantifiable

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/text/en -->
The 2021 study uses embryonic and hatchling Pterodaustro guinazui material to investigate early flight through glide predictions and humeral bending models. These support the authors’ early powered-flight interpretation, while fossils do not record takeoff itself and one model does not supply a complete life history.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Three specimens supply two hatchling models

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/7/text/en -->
Data from MHIN-UNSL-GEO-V 237, MHIN-UNSL-GEO-V 241 and embryo MMP 1168 supply two hatchling models with wingspans of about 0.244 and 0.290 m. A section from MMP 1168 supplies cortical thickness, while the other specimens supply shaft dimensions: no hatchling humerus provides all required inputs. The composites are not complete direct measurements of two individuals, and the two fixed database occurrence rows are not forcibly matched to these specimens.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Wing area and mass are estimated from scaling

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/text/en -->
Mass is estimated from wingspan scaling, wing area from a regression of 90 living bird species, and gliding from flight software. The authors acknowledge that the bird regression may miss details of pterosaur membranes, and set inputs such as span reduction and maximum lift coefficient. Area, mass and speed are model quantities rather than directly preserved soft tissues or recorded flights.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Glide distance and speed have model conditions

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/text/en -->
For the two Pterodaustro hatchling models, Table 2 predicts best-glide speeds of about 6.8 and 7.2 m/s and glide ratios of about 13.2 and 13.1. The ratio compares forward travel with height lost. These are neither measured maximum speeds nor proof of tree launching. Compared with the study’s adult model, hatchlings have lower predicted speeds and poorer long-distance glide performance; adult inputs do not establish the species’ maximum size.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Bending strength supports flight inference under material assumptions

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/text/en -->
Table 1 gives relative failure forces of about 14.32 and 12.91 times body weight for the two hatchling humeral models. Shafts are approximated as hollow circular tubes under cantilever loading, with bird-bone material parameters and shared hatchling/adult material properties. This supports the authors’ interpretation of capacity for launch and flight loading, without directly measuring flapping muscles, actual launch loads or fatigue damage. Models and sections were not independently reexamined here.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Ecological changes during growth remain hypotheses

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/11/text/en -->
The authors propose that growth changes speed, maneuverability and long-distance travel, potentially creating different opportunities for young and adults. They also require dietary and terrestrial-locomotion evidence. The models do not assign this species’ young to forests, adults to open land or establish an age-specific diet. Early flight does not exclude parental protection or feeding, which this study does not directly observe.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Compare modeled material with embryonic debate

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/12/text/en -->
Begin with how three specimens supply model inputs, then read predictions and their inferential scope. [Hamipterus tianshanensis](../../../Pteranodontoidea/Hamipterus/Hamipterus_tianshanensis/page.en.md) offers a route through embryonic staging, muscle crests and care: it is discussed in the paper, but these Pterodaustro values cannot become Hamipterus measurements. The 2021 author correction fixes two citation numbers without reporting changes to these model values.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Which fossil records this name represents

This guide uses 2 eligible species-level occurrence records from 2 collections and 2 reference identifiers. Occurrences, collections and references are different units, not specimen or individual counts.

## Age bounds of these records

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/15/text/en -->
Selected occurrences have an outer age envelope of 121.4–100.5 Ma, where Ma denotes millions of years ago. It combines the oldest early bound and youngest late bound among selected records, rather than a continuous record at every time or measured species origin, extinction or individual lifespan. Each collection retains its own age bracket. Broad intervals are not narrowed to precise specimen ages, and original dating interpretations are not revised here.

## Evidence limits

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/limitations/en -->
This page retains the fixed database occurrence scope and the material, assumptions and correction of the 2021 hatchling-flight models, adding text, table and captions from the 2008 growth and 2023 jaw-histology studies. Eleven MIC-V55 sections belong to one specimen. Attachment, tooth generations, diet and sexual maturity retain inferential limits. Images, specimens, sections and raw data were not independently examined, and models were not rerun. Original descriptions and subsequent literature remain incomplete. Database counts, ages, parent chains and maturity states remain; full reader delivery is pending.
