# Halszkaraptor escuilliei


## One holotype and competing ecological interpretations

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
Halszkaraptor escuilliei comes from the Upper Cretaceous of Mongolia. The 2020 study anchors its discussion to nearly complete holotype MPC-D 102/109, combining the author’s examination with synchrotron data acquired in 2016. Brownstein (2019) interprets much of its anatomy as retained maniraptoran ancestral traits and questions specialized semiaquatic fish eating. Cau (2020) responds character by character and supports derived amphibious adaptations. These are interpretations of the same fossil, not observations of living behavior; the 2019 paper does not conclude that the animal was herbivorous.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Compare snout openings, internal canals and their positions

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
The 2019 reassessment notes extensive snout foramina in other theropods and challenges treating their presence alone as an aquatic indicator. The 2020 response distinguishes the density and distribution of external openings, relative development of internal canals, and elongation and flattening of the premaxilla before the naris. Cau argues that this combination differs from widening around the nares in ornithomimosaurs and therizinosaurs. The comparisons use different anatomical resolutions. Shared openings do not by themselves establish shared ecology, and analogy does not measure this animal’s sensory performance or capture behavior.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## A replacement pattern is not a measured replacement rate

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
Brownstein compares delayed replacement in the original account with low replacement rates in other maniraptorans. Cau distinguishes the replacement pattern of premaxillary versus maxillary teeth from a claim about replacement speed; replacement waves and tooth spacing also require comparisons by jaw region. The response describes large premaxillary teeth, two reduced front maxillary teeth and larger teeth behind them, forming a varying-height oral margin interpreted as potentially useful for catching evasive prey. Anatomy supports a dietary hypothesis rather than direct food remains or a measured replacement interval in days.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## A long neck and a missing tail tip

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
The 2019 paper compares the long neck with other maniraptorans and interprets it as potentially ancestral. The 2020 response emphasizes character definitions and comparison groups: relative neck length and a particular form of cervical joint lamina are separate features, and a widespread structure may still have a distinctive development. The response explicitly notes that the distal tail of MPC-D 102/109 is missing and the total caudal count is unknown. A preserved tail segment does not establish a complete short tail. Neck proportions, full tail length, posture and diet require separate evidence.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Forelimbs and hollow bones enter the swimming debate

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
Brownstein questions aquatic life because the forelimb does not resemble a marine reptile paddle and lacks conspicuous cortical thickening. Cau replies that semiaquatic ecology need not require a plesiosaur-like paddle or universal bone thickening, comparing ulnar mid-shaft width relative to length with birds and emphasizing finger proportions. The response still notes that the fragmentary pectoral region prevents detailed reconstruction of forelimb range of motion; glenoid orientation partly rests on phylogenetic bracketing. Anatomical support for forelimb-assisted swimming does not establish a stroke, diving depth or speed.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Similar relationships do not automatically imply identical ecology

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
The 2019 analysis samples 150 taxa and finds Halszkaraptorinae sister to Unenlagiinae. The 2020 analysis of 185 units also supports that relationship, but reports that the 2019 supplied matrix does not reproduce its stated tree and challenges some character numbering and scores. This is Cau’s replication report, without independent reanalysis here. His 158-unit agreement subtree and ACCTRAN optimization classify 11 of 17 discussed body-plan features as lineage novelties, contrasting with Brownstein’s ancestral-trait interpretation. The 2020 author correction adds Figure 2 abbreviations and corrects Figure 6 panel letters; it does not adjudicate the ecological dispute.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Compare the added evidence from Natovenator

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/text/en -->
The 2022 Natovenator description uses articulated, backward-directed ribs to reconstruct its ribcage and supports a semiaquatic interpretation, extending that argument to Halszkaraptor through similarity. Read the [Natovenator polydontus specimen guide](../../Natovenator/Natovenator_polydontus/page.en.md) and compare its preserved ribs with the snout, tooth and forelimb arguments here. The added specimen is a comparative source rather than a behavioral record of Halszkaraptor or proof of uniform ecology across the subfamily.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Evidence limits

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/limitations/en -->
This is a guide to two named reassessments and a later comparison, not a complete monograph or adjudication of ecology. The complete 2017 description, all supplementary matrices and later literature are not assembled. No direct holotype examination, CT reconstruction or independent replication of the reported analyses occurred here. Anatomy, state optimization and ecological analogy retain distinct limits, without establishing diet, strokes, diving ability or full tail length. Database ages and depositional setting remain separate, without reclassification or external expert review.
