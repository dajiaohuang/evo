## Three populations assigned only tentatively
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc3345794-mdr4
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/text/en -->
The 2012 authors noted that the original nympha diagnosis relied largely on molecular characters and that comparable data were unavailable for most populations of the rufescens complex. They tentatively assigned Finca El Volcán in Guatemala and the San Pedro Sula and Santa Rosa del Copán areas in Honduras using proximity to the type locality and an earlier allozyme clustering result. These three population assignments remain tentative, rather than becoming independently confirmed contemporary nympha occurrences or additions to a certified range.

## Comparative statistics retain uncertain population identity
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/7/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc3345794-mdr4
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/7/text/en -->
Within the study’s assigned samples, males and females differed significantly in many measured dimensions and vomerine tooth counts. In the female analysis using three principal components, five of 14 nympha individuals were classified as occidentalis. The authors emphasized external resemblance among these small salamanders. These are results for the study’s operational groups, which included tentative populations; they do not establish universally separable morphology, a validated field identification rule or a false-identification rate for independently collected future specimens.

## Two mitochondrial markers and one nuclear locus define this study
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc3556321-mdr4
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/text/en -->
The 2012 study combined fieldwork from 2005–2011 with museum tissues, reporting ninety-six individuals from fifty-seven localities across its studied Nanotriton sample. Alignments were 522 bases for 16S, 772 for cytochrome b and 481 for nuclear POMC. POMC alleles were computationally phased; two distinct alleles from one animal are not two independent animals. Separate mitochondrial and nuclear gene trees generally supported four named lineages, but neither an individual locus nor this historical sample certifies every contemporary population or catalogue concept. Contact-zone sampling found no cross-species POMC-allele pairs or mitochondrial–POMC assignment discordance; this is no detected hybrid signature, not proof that hybridization is impossible.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## A 2012 reassignment expanded the studied geographic concept
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc3556321-mdr4
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/text/en -->
The paper assigned sampled populations from eastern Guatemala, Belize and Honduras, formerly included under rufescens, to nympha because their mitochondrial group included the nympha type locality. POMC also supported a named nympha lineage. This expands the source’s concept beyond the original Guatemala type-locality-only description, but it does not establish equivalence of every historical rufescens record or every contemporary nympha population. Confirmed sampled assignments should remain separate from a general regional prediction, especially where morphologically similar species approach one another.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Low sampled structure does not confirm a historical bottleneck
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc3556321-mdr4
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/text/en -->
Compared with rufescens and occidentalis, nympha showed lower haplotype diversity and weaker geographic structure, despite sampled occurrence across the Motagua–Polochic fault system. The Discussion proposes a historical range reduction or bottleneck and possible later expansion across that fault. It cites forest modelling at the Last Glacial Maximum as context, not a direct demographic test or a reconstructed continuous movement route. Failure to find a strong break in these markers does not show that the fault is universally permeable or that current populations lack genetic variation. Nor does a historical hypothesis establish a current decline.
<!-- /evo:text -->
