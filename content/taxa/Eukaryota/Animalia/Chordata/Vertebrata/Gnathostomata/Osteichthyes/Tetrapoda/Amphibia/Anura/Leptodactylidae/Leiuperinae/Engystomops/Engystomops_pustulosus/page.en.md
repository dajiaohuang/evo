## Dorsal skin temperature is an assumed internal-temperature proxy
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11383632-39tty
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/text/en -->
The Methods compare skin and cloacal temperatures ten times in nine animals from four species and report nonsignificant repeated-measures differences. The authors therefore treat skin temperature as representing internal body temperature. This is not an independently reproduced equivalence test, nor validation for all eleven sampled species, body sizes and field conditions. Six hourly ground-contact loggers sample forest/open conditions at three elevations, but logger positions are not identical to every frog’s substrate. The frog observations cover approximately 18:00–24:00, while plotted nightly logger summaries extend to 06:00; daytime and all-season thermal exposure remain outside this sample.

## Temperature associations do not establish heat-transfer mechanism
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11383632-39tty
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/text/en -->
The pooled results associate skin temperature more strongly with substrate than nearby air temperature. The authors propose thermoconformity and conductive heat exchange, but do not directly measure heat flux, evaporative loss or behavioral selection for this species. Analyses compare forest/open assemblages and elevations without a phylogenetic model; species composition and microhabitat use differ, so greater elevational association is not proof that ancestry has no influence. The printed Table 2 includes p < 0.000 entries and a low-elevation open-air p > 0.789 despite prose describing significance in all cases. Those probability statements remain unresolved rather than being silently corrected or reproduced as valid zero probabilities.

## Open-forest comparisons are bounded field observations
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11383632-39tty
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/text/en -->
For the pooled sample, open-habitat body temperatures are higher after size adjustment, but the within-elevation difference is significant only at low and middle elevations; it is nonsignificant at 2,700 m. Body-temperature variance also does not differ significantly by habitat. This is distinct from proving identical distributions or lower thermal tolerance in forest species. Forest canopy above 60% and open canopy up to 40% are source definitions, not every intermediate habitat or this species’ exclusive preference. The Conclusion proposes testing forest thermal specialization; specialization, vulnerability to warming and conservation outcomes were not experimentally established here.

## Repeat annotations are computational observations
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/13/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc12822217-39tty
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/13/text/en -->
The authors built species-specific repeat libraries with RepeatModeler2, classified unknown elements with RFSB and supplemented the libraries with vertebrate repeats before applying RepeatMasker. Its hits are termed insertions in the paper, but a hit is not independently demonstrated to be a complete, expressed or mobile element. Superfamily labels can aggregate distinct families. Element counts, total occupied length and genome proportions are separate quantities; overlapping hierarchy categories cannot safely be added as independent fractions. No new repeat annotation or expression experiment for Engystomops pustulosus was performed here.

## Across-species correlations do not prove this species mechanism
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/14/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc12822217-39tty
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/14/text/en -->
The study compared61 assemblies using phylogenetically adjusted models, an earlier pruned tree, contig-N50 covariates and multiple-comparison correction. Recently active Ty3/DIRS1-associated sequences correlate with genome size across that sample, but recent activity is a divergence-based proxy, not direct observation of current transposition in this species. Length and open-reading-frame similarity filters likewise do not demonstrate active protein expression. The authors' suggested developmental constraints are comparative hypotheses; they do not establish a causal TE effect on this species' larval duration or clutch size.
