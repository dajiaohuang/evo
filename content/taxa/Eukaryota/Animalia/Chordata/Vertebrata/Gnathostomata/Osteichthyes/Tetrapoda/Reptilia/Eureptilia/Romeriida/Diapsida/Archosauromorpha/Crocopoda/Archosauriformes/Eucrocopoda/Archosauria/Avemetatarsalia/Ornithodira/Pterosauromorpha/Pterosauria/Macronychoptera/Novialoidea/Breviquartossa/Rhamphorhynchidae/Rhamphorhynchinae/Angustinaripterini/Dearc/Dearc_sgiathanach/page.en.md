# Dearc sgiathanach


## A three-dimensional skeleton still constrained by limestone

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
Holotype NMS G.2021.6.1—4 comes from the Upper Lonfearn Member of the Lealt Shale Formation on Scotland’s Isle of Skye. The 2025 account distinguishes the main slab, skull block, ventral counterslab and separate wing-phalanx block within one associated skeleton. Delicate palatal, hyoid and neurocranial elements survive, the cervical series is complete and the right hand is particularly well preserved. Dorsals and ribs are disarticulated; pelvis, tail and hindlimbs are poorly preserved. Hard limestone obscures many surfaces, so three-dimensional preservation does not make the entire body observable.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## CT and bone landmarks provide different kinds of evidence

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
The authors micro-CT scanned the detached skull block and manually segmented internal spaces. Bone–matrix contrast is better in the rostrum and lower in the posterior skull, making segmentation harder there; brain and ear cavities are clearer. Muscle placement is separately inferred from ridges, depressions and textures using an extant phylogenetic bracket of crocodilians and birds. Scanned cavities, bone morphology and reconstructed muscles are distinct evidence: the fossil does not directly preserve this functioning muscle system or record actual flight.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Two tooth forms and wear support a feeding hypothesis

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
Preserved teeth include longer, recurved anterior fangs and short, straight, prism-like posterior teeth. Erosion particularly affects the front teeth, some surviving only as roots or mid-sections. Posterior crowns show chips, enamel cracking and spalling. Comparisons with Dorygnathus and other pterosaurs lead the authors to infer an ability to handle harder prey. Interlocking front teeth are also discussed as a possible fish-grabbing structure. No stomach contents or directly consumed animal is reported here; teeth do not establish an exclusively fish-based diet or fixed prey proportions.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Humeral crests and sternum extend the diagnosis

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
The authors propose two additional autapomorphies in 2025: a proximally expanding deltopectoral crest higher than the ulnar crest, and an anteriorly projecting sternal margin. The sternum is a thin, broad plate whose posterior part may not have fully ossified; poor preservation of other pterosaur sterna limits comparison. The humeri are exposed differently, and local flattening and fractures affect curvature, so left–right differences do not directly demonstrate living asymmetry. Crest morphology supports diagnostic and muscle-attachment comparisons without directly measuring muscle mass or flapping power.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Reconstructed flight muscles remain a hypothesis

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
The authors associate the expanded deltopectoral crest with pectoralis attachment and compare other humeral landmarks with latissimus dorsi and coracobrachialis attachments. They suggest flight may have relied more on humeral adduction and protraction. Differences from Rhamphorhynchus indicate that similarly sized pterosaurs need not share one flapping mechanism, but the authors explicitly present this as a hypothesis for future musculoskeletal modeling. Poor scapulocoracoid and metacarpal preservation leaves many attachments unclear; size effects, ancestry and convergence remain difficult to separate.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Curved hand claws suggest possible climbing

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
Digits I–III of the right hand are complete, while only part of wing digit IV survives. Unguals have developed flexor attachments and associated sesamoids. Comparing two-dimensional curvature with climbing and perching birds, the authors propose grasping or climbing and possibly a supplementary predatory role. Ungual bone is not a complete keratinous claw, and comparator functions do not directly record Dearc behavior. Frequency of tree use, climbing substrate and predatory claw use remain undetermined. Numerical lengths conflict with a later claim that the third ungual is longest, so no size ranking is imported.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## The stored placement and newer hypotheses coexist

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/text/en -->
The 2025 paper retains the Angustinaripterini terminology of the 2022 description while reporting another study including Skiphosoura that places Dearc closer to Monofenestrata. Relatively elongate cervicals and other traits may reflect modular evolution or size-related convergence; “transitional” does not establish direct ancestry. The fixed PBDB parent chain continues to organize these pages, with alternative hypotheses retaining their dates and attribution. Matrices were not rerun, and the 2022 original description and cited Skiphosoura paper were not independently read in full in this batch.
