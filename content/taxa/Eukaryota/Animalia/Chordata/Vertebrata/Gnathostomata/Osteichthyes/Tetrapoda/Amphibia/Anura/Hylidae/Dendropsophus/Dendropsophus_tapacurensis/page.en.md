## Accepted name and placement

COL26.8 accepts Dendropsophus tapacurensis Oliveira, Magalhães, Teixeira, Moura, Porto, Guimarães, Giaretta & Tinôco, 2021 as a species. Its recorded classification places it along Anura (order) → Hylidae (family) → Dendropsophus (genus). The name and placement refer to the release issued on 20 August 2026; names in another checklist or fossil database require their own identity evidence.

## Small body size and a frame-like colour pattern
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8279364-n9y52
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
The diagnosis reports males at 15.5–17.6 mm snout–vent length, mean 16.4 mm, and females at 19.5–20.7 mm, mean 20.1 mm. The variation account mentions two females, so the female range is not a broad population estimate. The head is wider than long; the single subgular vocal sac is light yellow in life; shanks bear transverse bars; and an irregular light-brown dorsolateral stripe runs from behind the eye toward the groin, with a similarly coloured triangular loreal mark. Male size overlaps the compared decipiens and haddadi samples, and body form resembles them. Identification therefore requires the full diagnostic combination, calls and sequence context rather than a size cutoff or frame-like pattern alone.

## Pigmentation varies and some bars fade in preservation
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8279364-n9y52
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
Type-series dorsal pigmentation ranged from mostly dark brown to pale yellow with scattered dark spots. The lighter dorsolateral stripe was almost always distinguishable but was not necessarily uniformly pigmented and could be regular rather than irregular. Shank bars varied in thickness and prominence and could disappear in preserved specimens. Some individuals had light spots beneath the eyes; both examined females had more clearly pointed digit discs than males. These sample observations distinguish live and preserved appearance and retain individual variation. They do not demonstrate colour-change mechanisms, the prevalence of each morph across the range or sex differences in every population.

## Two areas with unequal kinds of identity evidence
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8279364-n9y52
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
The account recorded tapacurensis at São Lourenço da Mata, Pernambuco, and Guaramiranga in the Baturité mountains, Ceará, about 590 km northwest of the type locality. The July 2018 type-area work concerned Buchada and Camocim Atlantic Forest fragments at 122 and 131 m elevation. The approximately 1,100 m figure describes the highland forest-enclave region, not the measured height of every Ceará frog. Crucially, the Ceará assignment was based on DNA sequences, and the authors requested morphological and acoustic confirmation. These records do not demonstrate a continuous occupied corridor, a newly observed migration or a fixed present range. Historical rainforest connections were proposed as a biogeographic explanation rather than directly reconstructed for this species.

## Leaf egg placement and prolonged rainy-season calling
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8279364-n9y52
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
The diagnosis explicitly states that eggs are laid on leaves overhanging temporary ponds. Natural-history observations found calling frogs on shrubs and marginal vegetation around sandy-bottomed temporary ponds 15–100 cm deep, with males commonly perched 10–150 cm above ground. Calling was heard throughout the reported April–August rainy-season activity period, usually around 18:00–23:00. The broader site-climate description gives a February–September rainy season; these are different scopes rather than interchangeable dates. Calling throughout a season does not mean each female breeds continuously. The text does not quantify clutch size, hatching success or parental attendance, and the recorded perches cannot all be treated as egg-bearing leaves.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## A note-centred description from six males
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8279364-n9y52
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/text/en -->
The call account used six males and described one pulsed note type, generally in series of three to nine notes and rarely isolated. Notes lasted 39–110 ms and contained nine to twenty-nine pulses; reported pulse repetition rate was 143–368 per second and dominant frequency 5,578–6,422 Hz. Eighty-three notes contributed to several summary measures, so that denominator is repeated notes rather than eighty-three frogs. Pulse duration and inter-note interval used different sample counts. The final pulse in a note was longer than preceding pulses. The analysis followed a note-centred approach, and note duration is not the duration of an entire multi-note series. These observations are not a measured complete repertoire or an invariant recognition threshold.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Supported affinity to oliveirai with mitochondrial limits
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/7/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8279364-n9y52
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/7/text/en -->
Bayesian and maximum-likelihood analyses recovered tapacurensis as sister to oliveirai, with posterior probability 1.0 and bootstrap 87. The dataset was mitochondrial H1, containing ribosomal segments and intervening tRNA, rather than a nuclear genomic assessment. Distances were separately calculated from a trimmed 400-bp 16S alignment: the paper reports at least 8% from examined group congeners and no more than 3% among its assigned tapacurensis populations. These percentages do not certify reproductive isolation or a universal species threshold. Methods contain inconsistent tissue/sequence sample totals, and results versus discussion differ in the stated early branching order of bromeliaceus and berthalutzae. Those discrepancies do not alter the separately reported tapacurensis–oliveirai sister relationship, but they remain unresolved source issues.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Historical haddadi records require renewed identification
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8279364-n9y52
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/text/en -->
The authors proposed that some Pernambuco and Alagoas records assigned to haddadi by external morphology might represent tapacurensis, while explicitly calling for acoustic and molecular examination. Those possible records are not added here as confirmed tapacurensis occurrences. The paper highlighted fragmented Atlantic Forest remnants and conservation units in the Pernambuco Endemism Center. Its cited estimate of only 11–16% of original Atlantic Forest cover remaining is a regional historical estimate, not a measured percentage loss of this frog population or a current site audit. The study did not certify a present Red List category, population trend or secure status merely because the type area is protected.
<!-- /evo:text -->
