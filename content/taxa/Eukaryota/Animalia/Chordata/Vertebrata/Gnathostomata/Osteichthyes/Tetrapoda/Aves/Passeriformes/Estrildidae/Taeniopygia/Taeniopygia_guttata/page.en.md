## evolution

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
Zebra finches are widely used to study learned song. Warren and colleagues' 2010 genome paper described the species as a model for vocal learning and analyzed gene expression relevant to the brain and song; this is context for the research, not a comprehensive field study of wild song behavior.

## evolution

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
The study sequenced and assembled a male zebra finch genome: about 1.2 billion bases in the draft assembly, with about 1.0 billion assigned to 33 chromosomes and three linkage groups. It also compared forebrain gene expression at 50 days after hatching, during the juvenile song-learning period, and at 850 days in adults; these two ages do not represent a complete developmental trajectory.

## evolution

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
Comparison with the chicken genome found broadly conserved synteny and karyotype, alongside many intrachromosomal rearrangements. This conclusion came from a comparison of two bird lineages at the time; it is neither a complete inventory of traits shared by all birds nor a substitute for later comparisons with more species and improved assemblies.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## catalogue-profile / limitations

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/limitations/en -->
This is a guide to one genome study, not a comprehensive zebra-finch account, and it is not externally expert-reviewed. The genome came from one male and the comparison was with chicken; the assembly, two sampled ages, and song-related molecular results are limited by that study design and its 2010 assembly, and do not represent species-wide geographic variation or a current reference assembly.
