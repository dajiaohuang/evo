## ecology

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
Henderson and colleagues monitored free-living blue tits at nest boxes near Loch Lomond, Scotland, across three breeding seasons. Higher parental baseline corticosterone was associated with lower temperatures, more rainfall, and lower territory-scale oak density. These are environmental associations within one study and do not show that any one factor alone caused a change in hormone levels.

## breeding

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
When breeding was out of sync with the caterpillar food peak, nestlings were lighter and fledging success was lower, but this timing mismatch was not associated with parental corticosterone. Among the environmental variables measured, only low temperature was associated with both lighter nestlings and higher parental corticosterone. The different measures did not follow one simple, shared relationship.

## physiology

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
In each study year, offspring of parents with higher corticosterone were lighter; yet in 2009 parental corticosterone was positively associated with the number of young fledged. The study therefore does not support treating corticosterone as a simple, general measure of breeding success. The authors emphasize interpreting this physiological measure alongside environmental conditions in each year.

## catalogue-profile / limitations

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/limitations/en -->
This is a local, single-study guide and is not externally expert-reviewed. The three years of observations came from one Scottish nest-box population and report associations; they do not establish that corticosterone or weather caused reproductive outcomes, or define general patterns across the blue tit's range, other life stages, or different years.
