## Size overlaps and limb-reach variation remain in the diagnosis
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc10563080-3xwdp
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/text/en -->
The revision reports adult males at 31–43.4 mm and females at 43.0–62.4 mm snout–vent length. The tibiotarsal articulation usually extends beyond the nostrils, rarely between eye and nostril; toe IV has 1.5–2 inner free phalanges, terminal disc width/base width is at least 1.70, and a pale upper lip stripe and prominent yellow inguinal patch are present. A comparison later calls the species at least 33 mm, inconsistent with the male minimum of 31 mm in the same paragraph. The discrepancy and limb variation prevent either value from becoming an absolute identification cutoff.

## River-bank encounters are mainly nocturnal
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc10563080-3xwdp
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/text/en -->
Femoralis is described as semi-aquatic around slow-flowing rainforest streams and rivers, rarely outside rainforest or near relict forest. Encounters span 09:00–23:30, with greater night activity and daytime concealment in holes or rock crevices. Females were generally on streamside shrubs at night; males used river-bank settings except branches and stems in the account. Observed heights reach one metre for males and two for females. The described stream-bed widths of 1–10 m and depths of 10–150 cm are observational habitat associations, not experimentally determined limits or requirements for every population.

## Egg placement and larval seasonality have locality-specific scope
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/11/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc10563080-3xwdp
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/11/text/en -->
Egg masses are described outside water on a leaf or branch overhanging a stream or river. The account reports cold-season reproduction, for example June–July at Mantadia, and apparent September–October metamorphosis in the Moramanga region. August 2002 larvae at Andranomanamponga occupied calm clear pools protected by rocks, 1–3 square metres and 20–100 cm deep; disturbed larvae retreated into mud, dead leaves or crevices. These locality-specific observations are not a universal calendar, clutch-size estimate, development-rate experiment or proof of parental guarding. Male calls are described only as low and barely audible, without measured acoustic parameters here.
