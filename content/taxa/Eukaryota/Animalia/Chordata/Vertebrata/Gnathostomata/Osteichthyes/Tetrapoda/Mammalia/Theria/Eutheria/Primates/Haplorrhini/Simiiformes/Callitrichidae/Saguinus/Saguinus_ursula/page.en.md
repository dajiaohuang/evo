## taxonomyAndIdentification

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
COL26.8 accepts Saguinus ursula (4TZC8) as a species. After revisiting the black-handed tamarin complex, Gregorin and de Vivo (2013) revalidated it using pelage differences, with molecular data corroborating the morphological divergence. In their samples, ursula east of the Tocantins River differed from S. niger west of it; the authors treated the forms as allopatric and the river as a possible barrier to gene flow. This summarizes a taxonomic comparison, not an independent test of the river-barrier hypothesis.

## typeAndStatus

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
MDD v2.5 lists the English common name Eastern Black-handed Tamarin, records lectotype ZMB 288 from near Belém, Brazil, and displays Not Evaluated. Brazil's ICMBio 2025 SALVE sheet uses Saguinus ursulus and explicitly lists S. ursula Hoffmannsegg, 1807 as an older name; its national category is Near Threatened (NT), with assessment date 27 September 2019. From estimated habitat loss of 14.5% in 2002–2020, future habitat models, and capture pressure, the assessment infers a possible reduction of about 30% over three generations. This is not a census-derived trend and is separate from MDD's field.

## fieldEcology

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/topic -->
fieldEcology
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
A study near Paragominas, Pará, followed one free-ranging group under the historical name Saguinus niger; ICMBio's assessment links the eastern-Amazonian taxon to current S. ursulus. The group used primary, selectively logged, and secondary forest; during the wet season it used more secondary forest and travelled shorter daily distances, which the authors related to fruit-source distribution. Scan samples in a separate local diet study were 87.5% fruit, 9.4% invertebrates, and 3.1% Parkia pendula exudate. The group fed on fruits from 18 plant species and swallowed seeds of six, indicating potential dispersal but not directly measuring forest-regeneration outcomes. These studies concern local groups.

## localAbundance

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/topic -->
localAbundance
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
In October 2013, researchers walked 320 km of transects in Gurupi Biological Reserve, Maranhão, and recorded 101 sightings of five primate species. S. ursulus was the most frequently encountered: about one group per 10 km. Groups contained 2–9 animals (mean 4.81); estimated density was 3.7 groups/km² (interval 2.15–6.37). These figures describe one reserve and a short survey, not range-wide abundance or a long-term trend.

## fieldParasiteStudy

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/topic -->
fieldParasiteStudy
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
A 2026 study of free-ranging tamarins in the eastern Brazilian Amazon detected enteroparasites in 13 of 17 capture events; *Prosthenorchis* was detected in 10. The study reported no clinical abnormalities. This is a parasite observation from one region and two sampling periods: an event-level proportion is not a population-wide infection rate or a population trend. It adds field health evidence, but cannot stand in for range-wide natural-history research.

## catalogue-profile / limitations

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/limitations/en -->
The field ecology, local density, and risk sources describe different places and periods: one focal group, one protected-area transect survey, and a national assessment. None is a synchronized range-wide census. The Tocantins as a barrier to gene flow is the original authors' interpretation, not a mechanism independently tested here. The Chinese name is editorial, and the page has not received external expert review.
