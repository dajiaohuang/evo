## A range account with a date attached
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/0/text/en -->
The 2016 IUCN assessment described a discontinuous range of more than 2.6 million km², from southern Senegal across the forest belt north of the Congo River to western Tanzania and western Uganda. It described four subspecies ranges. This is a dated assessment account, not a current range map.

## What one forest-edge study found
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/1/text/en -->
At Bossou, Guinea, researchers followed one wild western chimpanzee community for up to six hours a day from April 2012 to March 2013, including 10 adult focal individuals. Mature forest was the most selected habitat overall. Among feeding events in uncultivated habitat, records more than 200 m from fields exceeded chance expectations, while the 0–200 m bands fell below them. The authors suggested field-associated risks may shape feeding locations; this is a local result, not a species-wide habitat rule.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## A task-specific view of learning stick use
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/2/text/en -->
At Taï National Park in Côte d’Ivoire, a cross-sectional video study recorded 1,460 stick-use events from 70 wild western chimpanzees in three neighboring communities. In a model of grips used to insert sticks, the multi-digit grip became predominant at about 5.2 years, with a 98% predicted probability of use by age 15. Only three females from one community contributed observations above 45 years. The pattern describes this extractive-foraging task and sample; it is not a universal developmental curve or proof of a cognitive cause.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Growing up and first births at Gombe
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/3/text/en -->
Long-term records at Gombe National Park in western Tanzania covered 36 known-age wild females. Their mean age at sexual maturity was 11.5 years; mean age at first birth was 14.9 years. First-birth estimates came from a smaller, censored subset in which non-dispersing females were overrepresented, so they may be biased low. These are estimates for one population, not fixed values for every chimpanzee.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Why captive body-mass comparisons need context
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/4/text/en -->
A study compared 298 chimpanzees in African sanctuaries, 1,030 in zoos and 442 in research facilities. After age adjustment, adult animals in zoos and research facilities had greater body mass than sanctuary animals in both sexes; estimated growth was slower in the sanctuary cohort. The groups included mixed or unknown subspecies and different care settings, so this comparison cannot stand in for wild morphology or a subspecies comparison.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## A model of deep population history
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/5/text/en -->
A whole-genome site-pattern study sampled four chimpanzee lineages—central, eastern, Nigeria–Cameroon and western—alongside bonobos. Its best-fitting model estimated gene flow from western into eastern chimpanzees and placed the shared ancestor of the four sampled chimpanzee lineages at about 987,000 years ago. These are model-based population-history estimates with competing histories discussed by the authors, not dates from fossils.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## A regional conservation estimate, not a current census
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/6/text/en -->
A spatial model estimated 128,760 weaned central chimpanzees in Western Equatorial Africa in 2013 (95% CI 114,208–317,039); it estimated that 80.7% of this subspecies in the modeled region was outside the protected-area network. The result concerns one subspecies and a historical model period. The IUCN assessment linked above is version 2016-2, with errata published in 2018; its Endangered category is not presented as a verified current status.
