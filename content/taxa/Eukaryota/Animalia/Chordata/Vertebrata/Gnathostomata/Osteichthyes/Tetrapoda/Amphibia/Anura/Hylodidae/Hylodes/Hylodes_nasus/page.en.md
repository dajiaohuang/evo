## Paired-stage comparisons do not establish complete developmental independence
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/15/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11117230-3ng8t
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/15/text/en -->
Across the sampled species, larval–adult shape dissimilarities show a weak but significant Mantel association (r 0.16, p 0.003), while disparity correlations differ among families. The authors interpret the overall pattern as support for adaptive decoupling; it does not demonstrate zero correlation, genetic independence, an experimentally selected trait or this species’ developmental trajectory. Phylogenetic analyses use 74 of the 83 sampled species, and ancestral shape/time patterns are model reconstructions from extant data rather than newly measured fossil morphology. Adult K 0.44 with p 0.001 describes less close-relative similarity than the reference expectation; the paper’s “not phylogenetically structured” wording cannot mean that the test is nonsignificant. Supplement S3 repeats family N values summing to 112 for both stages, so adult individual denominators and imputed cells remain uncertified without raw matrices.

## Preserved material, finger numbering and staining
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/17/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8819055-3ng8t
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/17/text/en -->
No new specimens were collected for this study. Its dendrobatoid material comprised 77 specimens representing 36 source species labels; four additional specimens represented three hylodid species, and two Melanophryniscus outgroups were taken from literature. Exact preservation histories were unknown, although the authors understood the specimens to have been formalin-fixed and stored in ethanol. They usually removed one hand, decalcified it in EDTA and made 4.5 micrometre resin sections. Multiple stains tested tissue structure, neutral and acidic mucopolysaccharides, proteins and lipids. Fingers are numbered II–V under a tetrapod homology convention, so finger IV is the third of the four remaining digits. These methods describe the comparative study; they do not supply a species-specific sample size in the inspected main-text table. Appendix S1 and Tables S1–S2 remain unread, and figures were inspected only as captions.

## A proposed reproductive role remains untested
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/18/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8819055-3ng8t
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/18/text/en -->
The authors suggest that protein-containing specialized hand glands could mediate chemical courtship signals during contact with a female's head. They did not characterize the secretions, identify a pheromone, test female responses or establish whether delivery is olfactory or transdermal. Hand location and stain reactions therefore cannot certify that Hylodes nasus uses a particular chemical mating mechanism, causes ovulation or oviposition, or secretes an adhesive. A lack of these gland types likewise does not prove a species lacks every form of chemical communication. Behavioral observations cited in the discussion come from earlier studies and populations, not new mating experiments in this histological comparison.

## primaryStudyScopes / sectionEvidence / evidenceSummary

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/primaryStudyScopes/2/sectionEvidence/1/evidenceSummary/en -->
No new specimens were collected for this study. Its dendrobatoid material comprised 77 specimens representing 36 source species labels; four additional specimens represented three hylodid species, and two Melanophryniscus outgroups were taken from literature. Exact preservation histories were unknown, although the authors understood the specimens to have been formalin-fixed and stored in ethanol. They usually removed one hand, decalcified it in EDTA and made 4.5 micrometre resin sections. Multiple stains tested tissue structure, neutral and acidic mucopolysaccharides, proteins and lipids. Fingers are numbered II–V under a tetrapod homology convention, so finger IV is the third of the four remaining digits. These methods describe the comparative study; they do not supply a species-specific sample size in the inspected main-text table. Appendix S1 and Tables S1–S2 remain unread, and figures were inspected only as captions.

## primaryStudyScopes / sectionEvidence / evidenceSummary

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/primaryStudyScopes/2/sectionEvidence/2/evidenceSummary/en -->
The authors suggest that protein-containing specialized hand glands could mediate chemical courtship signals during contact with a female's head. They did not characterize the secretions, identify a pheromone, test female responses or establish whether delivery is olfactory or transdermal. Hand location and stain reactions therefore cannot certify that Hylodes nasus uses a particular chemical mating mechanism, causes ovulation or oviposition, or secretes an adhesive. A lack of these gland types likewise does not prove a species lacks every form of chemical communication. Behavioral observations cited in the discussion come from earlier studies and populations, not new mating experiments in this histological comparison.
