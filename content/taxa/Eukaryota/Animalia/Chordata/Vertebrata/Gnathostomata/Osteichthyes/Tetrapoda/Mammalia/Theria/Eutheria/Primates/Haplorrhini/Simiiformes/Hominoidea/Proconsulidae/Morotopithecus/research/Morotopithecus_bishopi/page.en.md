# Morotopithecus bishopi

## atlas-profile / overview

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/overview -->
Separate shoulder and femoral specimens MUZM 60 and MUZM 80 from Moroto I and II provide a comparative test of early-Miocene hominoid locomotion; they are not an associated skeleton or demonstrated crown-ape ancestor.

## Shoulder and thigh from separate finds
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/0/text/en -->
The Moroto sample discussed in the paper is built from separate fossils: shoulder material MUZM 60 from Moroto I and a femur, MUZM 80, from Moroto II in Uganda. They were not found as one associated skeleton. The authors compare each bone with other primates and use the locality record to frame their interpretation, keeping specimen identity distinct from the broader species name Morotopithecus bishopi.

## What the postcranial bones suggest
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/1/text/en -->
The study uses shoulder and femoral form to test how Morotopithecus may have moved. Its comparative functional analysis supports a mixed arboreal repertoire rather than a simple label based on one bone. This is a reconstruction from preserved anatomy and comparison with living and fossil primates; no one observed the extinct animal moving, and the bones do not directly record its day-to-day behavior.

## Function does not supply a family tree
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/2/text/en -->
A locomotor interpretation answers a different question from phylogenetic placement. The two postcranial specimens do not form a complete skeleton, directly establish diet or habitat, or show that Morotopithecus was a direct ancestor of living apes. The paper’s conclusions should be read as a functional model for a bounded fossil sample, with missing anatomy left open.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## atlas-profile / readerLimitations

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerLimitations/en -->
This guide summarizes a functional study of two separate postcranial specimens. It is not a complete skeleton, observed behavior, species-wide ecological account, or demonstration of direct ancestry.
