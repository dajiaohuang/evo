# Tropeognathus mesembrinus


## Grey preserved regions within a complete reconstructed outline

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
Figure 1 in the 2013 study shows separate cranial and mandibular fragments; Figure 2 reconstructs a skull outline with preserved parts explicitly in grey. Read the grey areas before the outline rather than treating reconstructed rostral tips, opening margins or teeth as entirely preserved. Figure 3 arrows identify surface channels interpreted in the caption as vascular impressions. The photograph documents channels while vascular attribution and cooling remain interpretive steps. No new measurements were taken from the scale bars.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Why the large specimen retains a cf. identification

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
The 2013 study identifies MN 6594-V as Tropeognathus cf. T. mesembrinus. Cranial features support comparison with the species, while cf. retains uncertainty. The authors discuss individual variation or growth as possible explanations for crest differences without excluding other interpretations. The original holotype is principally cranial material and supplies no associated limb proportions. This species guide retains the comparative identification rather than creating an unqualified species occurrence.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Concretion preservation does not recover the exact collecting site

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
MN 6594-V preserves cranial and mandibular material, part of the vertebral column, girdles, sternum, pelvis and wing elements in concretions, with only one femur representing the hindlimbs. Local people collected it earlier and its precise site is unknown. Nodule characteristics support a Romualdo origin, with the Santana do Cariri area proposed as a probable source. Calcite veins, weathering and compression displaced or deformed parts, and the rostral tip is missing. A probable origin does not supply coordinates or an intact, undeformed three-dimensional body.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Crests and alveoli preserve form while function remains interpretive

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
Premaxillary-crest inclination differs from the holotype, while a low blunt frontoparietal crest, occipital orientation and palatal ridge contribute to the authors’ comparison. Surface grooves are interpreted as vascular impressions, with cooling a functional hypothesis rather than preserved thermoregulatory behavior. Cranial damage leaves openings and tooth rows incomplete; visible alveoli do not establish a complete tooth count or diet.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## The preserved body records support and missing segments

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
The authors record twelve dorsal vertebrae, including five centra fused into a notarium while some neural spines remain unfused. Five sacrals join the ilium; the scapula and coracoid and carpal series are fused. Humeral and forearm shafts are incomplete, only the proximal wing metacarpal remains, and the pelvis is incomplete. These observations inform comparisons of joints and trunk support without supplying a wholly measured wing-bone chain or a universal developmental sequence.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Sampled histology supports growth approaching cessation

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
Histology samples the distal portion of the first wing phalanx of MN 6594-V, with calcite obscuring some structures. The authors describe an external fundamental system, parallel circumferential tissue and growth marks, interpreting growth as ceased or close to cessation despite incomplete fusion in some axial structures. A local section is not a continuous whole-body growth record; growth marks do not establish exact age or maximum adult size for the species.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## The 8.70 m wingspan includes comparative reconstruction

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/text/en -->
The study reconstructs missing segments from preserved bone-end dimensions and limb proportions of other pterosaurs, combining wing, carpal and girdle lengths. Its mean “maximized wingspan” is about 8.70 m and “normal wingspan” about 8.26 m, the latter using a proposed 5% reduction for joint flexion. These are specimen model estimates rather than measurements between intact wingtips or a species maximum. Comparator identities and proportional assumptions affect the result; 5% is not a universal rule for pterosaurs.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Other large wing bones retain family-level uncertainty

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/7/text/en -->
MCT 1838-R is an isolated distal humerus and MPSC R 1395 a partial left wing in the same study. Both retain indeterminate Anhangueridae identifications rather than becoming Tropeognathus mesembrinus because of size. An unfused wing-finger extensor process in MPSC material informs the authors’ discussion of immaturity. Different preserved elements and growth stages cannot be assembled into an ideal adult or extend this species’ size range using the largest estimate.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## The Gondwana size claim retains its 2013 comparison scope

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/text/en -->
The title’s largest flying reptile from Gondwana claim belongs to the 2013 material and comparison context. The paper uses a wingspan over 7.4 m as an operational giant threshold and explicitly acknowledges its arbitrary choice. This is neither a biological clade nor a current independently checked record. Preservation and collection of a few large specimens do not establish population abundance or coastal habitat preference.
<!-- /evo:text -->
