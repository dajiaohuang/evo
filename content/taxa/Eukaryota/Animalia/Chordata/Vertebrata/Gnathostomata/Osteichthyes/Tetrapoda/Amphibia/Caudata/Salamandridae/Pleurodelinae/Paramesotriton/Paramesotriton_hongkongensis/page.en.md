## Historical stream samples and seven retained microsatellites
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/11/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11974448-4d374
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/11/text/en -->
The 2025 paper analysed 310 newts sampled from ten second- or third-order streams with breeding pools in Hong Kong SAR between August 2013 and January 2014. Seven streams were in the mainland New Territories and three on islands. Each stream was treated as a population because drainage basins differed and the minimum distance between sites was approximately two kilometres. Samples numbered 30–35 animals per site. Nine microsatellite loci were initially amplified; Phon24 and Phon48 were excluded after null alleles were detected, leaving seven for analysis. This is a historical ten-stream genetic sample, not a contemporary census, complete species range or nine-locus final dataset. The paper withholds precise collecting locations because of wildlife-trade concerns.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Diversity and bottleneck signals describe the sampled markers
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/12/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11974448-4d374
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/12/text/en -->
Across retained markers, reported observed heterozygosity ranged from 0.631 to 0.814 (mean 0.748, SD 0.088), and expected heterozygosity from 0.706 to 0.890 (mean 0.839, SD 0.030). Site averages for allele number, allelic richness and observed heterozygosity did not differ significantly; expected heterozygosity was significantly lower only at TPK. The source reports bottleneck evidence at PNS and SM, two of ten sites, using heterozygosity-excess tests under specified stepwise-mutation settings. These results do not measure current abundance, fitness or whole-genome diversity, and nondetection of a bottleneck elsewhere does not prove demographic stability. High marker diversity despite disturbance does not establish that urbanization has no harmful effects.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Four principal genetic clusters are analytical management leads
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/13/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11974448-4d374
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/13/text/en -->
Bayesian clustering selected K = 4 as the principal partition: SP alone, PFL alone, a group of six mainland New Territories populations plus one Hong Kong Island population, and TPK alone. The source also reports some support for K = 9, so four clusters are not an immutable biological count. Most populations showed admixture, compatible with gene flow and shared ancestry rather than complete isolation. AMOVA assigned 92.3% of variation within sites, 2.0% among clusters and 5.7% among populations within clusters. These microsatellite partitions do not by themselves delimit new species or prove adaptive differentiation; their proposed conservation use remains an interpretation of sampled structure.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Genetic differentiation is not a measured migration rate
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/14/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11974448-4d374
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/14/text/en -->
Pairwise FST ranged from 0.003 for PNS versus SM to 0.172 for SP versus TPK; the reported pairwise mean was 0.067, SD 0.038. All pairs except PNS–SM remained significantly above zero after correction. The isolation-by-distance test was nonsignificant (R² = 0.08, p = 0.16), using straight-line site distances. This does not show that distance never matters, that every population exchanges migrants, or a quantified number of migrants per generation. Admixture may reflect shared ancestry or recent/ongoing exchange, and the authors acknowledge that only ten sites were sampled. Land cover, slope, roads and physiological constraints remain further research questions.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Marker contrasts and unusual assignments leave historical causes open
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/15/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11974448-4d374
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/15/text/en -->
A previous three-site AFLP study suggested that a mountain ridge separated two mainland populations more strongly than a sea barrier separated an island population. In this microsatellite study those two mainland sites, PNS and SM, instead had the lowest measured pairwise differentiation, and site elevation did not correlate with diversity. The authors attribute the contrast partly to different marker properties and sampling limitations, rather than certifying one universal barrier rule. TT grouped with mainly mainland populations, while TPK formed its own cluster. Translocation or releases are proposed possible explanations; genetic clustering does not establish the origin or release history of these populations.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Seasonal breeding movements are cited separately from genotyping
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/16/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11974448-4d374
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/16/text/en -->
The Discussion cites earlier tracking and demographic work describing adults moving between terrestrial habitat and stream breeding pools, repeated returns of known individuals to the same pool, and terrestrial displacement approaching 0.4 km. Some individuals may move between pools or streams. These are attributed earlier observations, not movements measured from the present tail-clip samples, a universal dispersal maximum or an independently tested cause of the observed diversity. The source explicitly leaves unknown why an individual returns to the same pool and how such behaviour maintains genetic variation.

## Genetically informed relocation is proposed; road effects were not tested
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/17/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc11974448-4d374
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/17/text/en -->
The paper recommends considering genetic composition when relocating, translocating or reintroducing newts, treating its four principal clusters as distinct management units while retaining connectivity between mainland clusters. This is a research recommendation, not a tested relocation protocol or a current legal designation. It also describes substantial road mortality in terrestrial nonbreeding habitat, but states that the precise effects of roads on gene flow were not evaluated here. The study does not provide a newly verified conservation category, current population trend or evidence that high microsatellite diversity removes habitat and collection threats.

In this microsatellite study those two mainland sites, PNS and SM, instead had the lowest measured pairwise differentiation, and site elevation did not correlate with diversity. The authors attribute the contrast partly to different marker properties and sampling limitations, rather than certifying one universal barrier rule. TT grouped with mainly mainland populations, while TPK formed its own cluster. Translocation or releases are proposed possible explanations; genetic clustering does not establish the origin or release history of these populations.

The Discussion cites earlier tracking and demographic work describing adults moving between terrestrial habitat and stream breeding pools, repeated returns of known individuals to the same pool, and terrestrial displacement approaching 0.4 km. Some individuals may move between pools or streams. These are attributed earlier observations, not movements measured from the present tail-clip samples, a universal dispersal maximum or an independently tested cause of the observed diversity. The source explicitly leaves unknown why an individual returns to the same pool and how such behaviour maintains genetic variation.

The paper recommends considering genetic composition when relocating, translocating or reintroducing newts, treating its four principal clusters as distinct management units while retaining connectivity between mainland clusters. This is a research recommendation, not a tested relocation protocol or a current legal designation. It also describes substantial road mortality in terrestrial nonbreeding habitat, but states that the precise effects of roads on gene flow were not evaluated here. The study does not provide a newly verified conservation category, current population trend or evidence that high microsatellite diversity removes habitat and collection threats.
