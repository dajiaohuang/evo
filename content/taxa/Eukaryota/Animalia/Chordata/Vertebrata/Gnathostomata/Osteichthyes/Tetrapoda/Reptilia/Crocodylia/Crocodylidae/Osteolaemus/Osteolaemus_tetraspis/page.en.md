## Read the study

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
How should cave ecology be read alongside a changing geographic-lineage framework? This guide follows the accepted COL26.8 record 6TBCB and reads the study at its stated sample and geographic scale. A Gabon study treated dwarf crocodiles from the Abanda cave system and surrounding forest as Osteolaemus tetraspis. Cave animals ate mainly cave crickets and bats; forest animals ate mainly freshwater crustaceans and insects, and cave prey was not found in the forest animals examined. Juvenile cave crocodiles had higher body condition than forest juveniles. The adult difference was not statistically significant, and the authors noted that small sample size may have limited that comparison.

Continue with Osteolaemus osborni (COL 8SBC9) to compare the cave study with a separate shared-site genomic sample while retaining both sample boundaries. The Crocodylia group guide provides the wider reading route; the neighboring page retains its own sources and limits.

## Read the study

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
A multilocus analysis of African dwarf crocodiles from known localities supported three deeply divergent geographic lineages in the Congo Basin, Ogooué Basin and West Africa. The type specimen of O. tetraspis came from the Ogooué River basin in Gabon, and the study emphasized that lineage boundaries require multiple kinds of evidence. The cave-ecology study used the name O. tetraspis; this guide neither raises the Abanda cave population to a separate species nor treats it as representing the full range and ecology of the COL accepted species.
