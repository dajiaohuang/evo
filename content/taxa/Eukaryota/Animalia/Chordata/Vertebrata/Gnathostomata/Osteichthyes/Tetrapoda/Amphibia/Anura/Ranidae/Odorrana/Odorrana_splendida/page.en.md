## A stream larva with a potentially long development period
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc6366209-48sgr
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
The 2019 study targeted the tadpoles of splendida, described as endemic to Amami-Oshima Island, Japan. Its study-species background cited earlier work for February–April breeding, eggs placed in rock crevices or water flowing beneath rocky headstreams, larval periods sometimes exceeding two years, and metamorphosis from late June to early September. It also cited tadpoles reaching about 1.85 cm snout–vent length and approximately one gram wet mass. These are attributed background records, not new longitudinal measurements made by this eDNA project, and they do not mean every individual takes more than two years or metamorphoses on identical dates. Snout–vent length excludes the tail; the cited local density and dispersal estimates cannot be generalized to every stream.

## Detection in known occupied streams can still miss tadpoles
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc6366209-48sgr
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
Researchers collected fifty-three water samples at ten previously known occupied headwater streams in 2015 and 2017. Environmental DNA was detected in forty-two samples, or 79.2%, while visual searches within ten metres upstream detected tadpoles in forty-nine samples, or 92.5%. Each water sample had eight qPCR replicates; any positive well made the sample positive. These denominators are samples, not independent populations or an island-wide occupancy estimate. Controls were negative, but a negative environmental sample did not prove absence: thirty tadpoles were counted in one pool without a positive DNA result. The discussion states 22.6% negatives, which differs from eleven out of fifty-three (20.8%) implied by the results; the discrepancy is retained rather than silently corrected in the source account.

## DNA flux correlates with abundance but is not a reliable head count
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc6366209-48sgr
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
The study multiplied DNA concentration in copies per litre by stream discharge in litres per second to obtain flux in copies per second. This differs from concentration alone. Combined-site regressions were positive, with r-squared 0.32 for tadpole abundance and 0.29 for estimated biomass, yet the authors concluded that their sampling regime did not accurately estimate abundance. Counts concerned the ten metres upstream; biomass was inferred from photographed snout–vent lengths using an allometric equation based on an earlier, unpublished set of 468 tadpoles, rather than weighing every animal. Similar counts could produce very different fluxes among streams and dates. DNA transport, retention and uneven sampling therefore prevent a universal conversion from a positive signal or a copy number to local frog density.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Seasonal signal and sampling improvements need local interpretation
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc6366209-48sgr
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
Among seven streams used for seasonal comparison, peak DNA flux occurred in May at two sites, July at one and August at four. Sampling was uneven because time constraints and heavy rain prevented access, so the pattern is not a synchronized island-wide reproductive peak or evidence that populations rose and fell in proportion to DNA. The paper suggested greater water volume or more subsamples to improve detection and estimation, while explicitly requiring further validation. A four-sample inhibition check supported low inhibition in those tested samples, not proof that every negative sample was inhibition-free. Habitat degradation and introduced-predator concerns and the Endangered label were cited in the 2019 introduction; this study did not independently reassess current conservation status or quantify predator-caused decline.
