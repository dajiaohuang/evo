## Seven males and four females have overlapping adult sizes
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq83l
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
The examined adult males measure 13.1–17.0 mm snout–vent length, n=7, and females 14.8–16.9 mm, n=4; two fresh metamorphs at 7.4–8.7 mm are separate developmental samples. Male throat asperities form a central stripe widening anteriorly. Rudimentary toe webbing, a covered inconspicuous tympanum, absent eyelid cornicles and comparatively small size contribute to the diagnosis. Maxillary and premaxillary teeth are present, vomerine teeth absent. Neither overlapping size nor one character identifies every specimen; juvenile steindachneri can be superficially similar and requires comparative examination.

## Live markings vary within the examined series
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq83l
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
The male holotype has dark brown dorsal colour with a broad light brown stripe edged in black and a finer vertebral line, plus scattered orange and white spots. Its throat is dark with white asperities, belly yellow to cream and hind-limb underside pink-orange. The wider series varies in dorsal stripes, spots and interorbital colour; female thighs are generally more vividly orange and female throats lighter than males. These are specimen and sex-associated observations, not invariant markings, a toxicity measurement or warning-colour experiment. Preserved colours remain distinct from photographed live appearance.

## Larval morphology does not establish a growth trajectory
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq83l
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/text/en -->
The described larvae have an ovoid body, rounded snout, dorsolateral eyes, a left spiracle and right vent tube. The anteroventral oral disc has papillae along the lower labium but not the upper, with keratodont formula 1/1 + 1//2 as printed and finely serrated jaw sheaths. Body and tail are dark above, the underside unpigmented and fins mostly transparent. The authors qualify the rounded tail tip as possibly unnatural. ## Barcode and longer-fragment distances answer different comparisons

Six sequenced arcanus specimens contain two 510-base 16S haplotypes, three specimens each, differing by two substitutions. Distance to mbabo is 1.2% in this barcode but 5.0% in the longer 12S–16S fragment; these marker scopes are not interchangeable. Six arcanus specimens yield eight inferred nuclear RAG1 haplotypes, none shared with mbabo or the other examined species. Inferred allelic haplotypes are not eight independent frogs. ## Notes, groups and whole calls retain different durations
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq83l
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/text/en -->
Afternoon advertisement calls recorded on 5 November 2009 consist of metallic clicking notes; the whole call can last seconds to over a minute. At the reported 17.5 °C, note groups average 1.7 ± 0.1 s and contain 6–8 notes; notes average 12.5 ± 0.1 ms, repetition rate 4.2 ± 0.7 per second and dominant frequency 3.92 ± 0.03 kHz. The source explicitly uses mean ± standard deviation. Repetition accelerates within a call and the group’s last note has higher pitch. These summaries do not turn group duration into whole-call duration or repeated notes into independent frogs, nor establish function beyond the attributed advertisement description.

## Daylight pond encounters support an apparent activity tendency
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq83l
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/text/en -->
Most observed frogs were jumping in herbaceous vegetation by a small stream pond outside forest, among shrubs and montane grassland. One male was found at night near a stream in escarpment forest. Males called from partly submerged plants with leaves above water and larvae occurred in the pond. The authors infer relatively diurnal activity and possible open-habitat preference; this is not a controlled preference or complete daily schedule. In June 2016 calls at another site were heard but not recorded and no animals seen, so their attribution remains probable rather than a confirmed voucher record.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Two Gangirwal sites remain distinct from uncertain older localization
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq83l
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/text/en -->
The source confirms two Mount Gangirwal sites in the Gotel Mountains on the Cameroon–Nigeria border at 1,940 and 2,250 m, about 1.1 km apart by air. The holotype is from the 2,250 m pond site. A 1988 record published under Phrynobatrachus sp. is not precisely located; its coordinates and 2,300 m label roughly correspond to the studied area. These different label scopes are retained, rather than pooling every value into a verified continuous elevation range. Border-area records do not independently establish occupancy of all neighbouring mountain habitats.

## The authors recommend Critically Endangered from limited dated evidence
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/11/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq83l
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/11/text/en -->
The 2020 account strongly recommends Critically Endangered, citing estimated occupancy below 10 km², extent below 100 km², one location and presumed decline. In the 2016 Gotel survey no Phrynobatrachus specimen was found, although arcanus was possibly heard; small size and dense vegetation were noted as detection difficulties. Non-detection is not a quantified species-specific decline or proof of extinction. The recommendation and area estimates are the study’s dated evidence, not a current independently verified IUCN assessment or a new complete range calculation.
