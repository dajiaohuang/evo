## The examined Peruvian population has a provisional species name
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8819055-3ngxs
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/text/en -->
The study retained the name Hyloxalus nexipus for its Peruvian population, while noting that an earlier analysis called those specimens H. aff. nexipus and suggested they were not conspecific with the nominal Ecuadorian population. The authors followed a broader name pending clarification, based on a later polytomy involving those samples. Their hand observations therefore cannot be certified here as attributes of the narrow accepted species simply because the name matches. The source's population-level gland scores remain in the evidence material, without a narrow-species phenotype assignment.

## Preserved material, finger numbering and staining
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/11/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc8819055-3ngxs
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/11/text/en -->
No new specimens were collected for this study. Its dendrobatoid material comprised 77 specimens representing 36 source species labels; four additional specimens represented three hylodid species, and two Melanophryniscus outgroups were taken from literature. Exact preservation histories were unknown, although the authors understood the specimens to have been formalin-fixed and stored in ethanol. They usually removed one hand, decalcified it in EDTA and made 4.5 micrometre resin sections. Multiple stains tested tissue structure, neutral and acidic mucopolysaccharides, proteins and lipids. Fingers are numbered II–V under a tetrapod homology convention, so finger IV is the third of the four remaining digits. These methods describe the comparative study; they do not supply a species-specific sample size in the inspected main-text table. Appendix S1 and Tables S1–S2 remain unread, and figures were inspected only as captions.

## A proposed reproductive role remains untested

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/12/sourceIds/0 -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/12/text/en -->
The authors suggest that protein-containing specialized hand glands could mediate chemical courtship signals during contact with a female's head. They did not characterize the secretions, identify a pheromone, test female responses or establish whether delivery is olfactory or transdermal. Hand location and stain reactions therefore cannot certify that Hyloxalus nexipus uses a particular chemical mating mechanism, causes ovulation or oviposition, or secretes an adhesive. A lack of these gland types likewise does not prove a species lacks every form of chemical communication.
