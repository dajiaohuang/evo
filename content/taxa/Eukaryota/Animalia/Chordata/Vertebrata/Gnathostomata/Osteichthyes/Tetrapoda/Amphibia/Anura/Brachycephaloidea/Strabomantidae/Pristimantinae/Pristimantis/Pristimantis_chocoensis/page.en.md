## An amplectant pair inside an abandoned termite nest
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc10719609-scd85
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
At 02:16 on 3 June 2022, observers found a Pristimantis chocoensis pair already in amplexus inside a degraded abandoned termite nest attached to a fallen trunk in Río Canandé Reserve, Esmeraldas, Ecuador, at 350 m elevation. The nest had a large upper opening and loose soil. The pair occupied a depression for nearly two hours before observed egg and sperm release at 04:02. Because amplexus had begun before discovery, the observers could not determine whether either adult had dug the depression. This is one observed site and event, not proof that termite nests are obligatory breeding sites, an annual breeding calendar or a measured altitude limit.

## Egg rotation followed by active covering with soil
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc10719609-scd85
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
The female released at least twenty eggs, a lower-bound observation rather than an exact or species-typical clutch size. For approximately an hour she remained over the eggs, rotated her body and used alternating hind-leg scrambling movements to mix and coat them with soil. At 05:14 she was seen pulling surrounding loose soil underneath her body until the eggs were completely buried in a mound. The activity continued at least until 06:10; its exact end was not recorded. The male stayed nearby, immobile and not calling during this process. Apparent additional egg release and abdominal contractions were interpreted by the observers, without a measured fertilization-success result.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Short female attendance and uncertain male guarding
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc10719609-scd85
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
At 15:00 on the same day both adults rested near their earlier positions, with the female on the egg mound. On the second night the male was no longer visible at or immediately around the site, while the female remained on the mound; neither adult was seen there during the following seven nights. The authors summarized attendance as approximately twenty-four hours for the female and twelve for the male, interpreting the female as guarding but explicitly unable to confirm whether the male’s proximity was intended care or incidental. These observations do not document continuous monitoring, hatching success, survival of the buried eggs or a universal duration of parental care.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Proposed functions of burying remain hypotheses
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc10719609-scd85
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
The report interpreted egg burying and brief female attendance in the context of direct development and terrestrial reproduction. It proposed that rotation could distribute sperm across the clutch, and discussed concealment and ultraviolet protection as potential benefits of soil covering. It also suggested an allocation shift toward intense brief nest care rather than prolonged guarding. No sperm-distribution measurement, predator-exclusion experiment, ultraviolet exposure assay or comparison of hatchling survival was performed for this observed event. The behavioural record supports that the female actively buried eggs; its adaptive functions and generality within the species require further study.
