# Alanqa saharica


## The 2023 study returns to a lower-jaw interpretation

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
FSAC-KK 26 retains its specimen identity: the 2010 naming paper interprets it as a lower jaw, the 2020 study as a rostrum, and Smith and colleagues in 2023 tentatively return to a lower jaw. Their comparison interprets the posterior modification as paired projections that do not meet anteriorly, while referring FSAC-KK 5204 and 5205, with a single median boss, as upper jaws. Cross sections, foramina and lateral angles contribute to that distinction. The three dated interpretations remain visible; the tentative 2023 interpretation is not a new specimen identification made here or a second holotype.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Separate revised jaw referrals from isolated body bones

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
The 2023 account expands jaw comparisons referred to Alanqa and revisits historical referrals. It identifies FSAC-KK 28 as indeterminate Azhdarchoidea jaw morphotype A. Cervicals CMN 50801, LINHM 014 and FSAC-KK 5077, previously referred to Alanqa in earlier work, remain indeterminate Azhdarchidae in this discussion; humerus CMN 50814 is indeterminate Azhdarchoidea. Without associated material, isolated body bones cannot be assigned with certainty to a genus or species named from jaws. UOP-PAL KK 0006 and FSAC-KK 5213 are two three-dimensional print models of one privately held specimen, not two original individuals.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Paired projections affect comparison without resolving the tree

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
The 2023 authors argue that the two posterior projections on the Alanqa type do not meet anteriorly, questioning comparisons that treat them as one V-shaped ridge in earlier relationship hypotheses. They retain Alanqa in Azhdarchidae and discuss how analyses treated its upper- or lower-jaw identity. The 2020 and 2023 accounts differ in describing the identity used by one earlier analysis; those reports cannot stand in for inspection of its matrix coding. This guide presents the 2023 argument and disagreement without rerunning phylogenetic analyses or claiming a complete review of subsequent studies or a settled classification.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## One type specimen, two dated anatomical interpretations

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
The holotype of Alanqa saharica is FSAC-KK 26, housed at the Faculté des Sciences Ain Chock, Université Hassan II, Casablanca, Morocco. The 2010 naming paper interprets it as an almost complete mandibular symphysis. The 2020 Kem Kem study reinterprets the same material as a rostrum and records in-situ collection at Aferdou N’Chaft in 2008. The type identity is retained while anatomical interpretations are separated by date; reinterpretation does not create a second specimen or occurrence.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Three adjoining jaw fragments retain missing portions

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
The 2010 description records three adjoining orange-brown fragments with a combined preserved length of 344 mm, missing the anterior tip and part of the ventral intermediate fragment. Extending the margins and comparing related forms suggests up to 80 mm of missing tip; that is reconstruction, not preserved length. Three-dimensional form and thin bone walls help compare cross sections, but the material is neither a complete skull nor an associated head-and-body skeleton.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Straight toothless margins and interpretations of the boss

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
The 2010 authors describe the taxon as toothless from absent alveoli in the five assigned jaw specimens, emphasizing straight margins, slender form and a cross section changing from near-triangular to Y-shaped. A posterior prominence on the type was interpreted as a V-shaped midline structure of the mandibular symphysis. After the 2020 rostral reinterpretation, the discussion concerns a palatal boss and corresponding mandibular occlusal surfaces. Foramina and grooves inform a proposed vascular and neural supply to a beak sheath; they do not preserve a complete sheath or establish a diet.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Historically referred pieces are not one associated skull

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/text/en -->
The 2010 paper additionally refers BSP 1996 I 36, FSAC-KK 31, BSP 1993 IX 338 and FSAC-KK 27. FSAC-KK 31 comes from Boumerade and FSAC-KK 27 from Aferdou N’Chaft, whereas the BSP provenance is only “possibly near Taouz.” Attribution compares morphology and size. A figure matches the type with another rostral specimen for jaw reconstruction, not as demonstrated parts of one individual. The later anatomical reinterpretation of the type also requires reading those historical upper/lower-jaw matches in their source context.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## The cervical fragment retains indeterminate attribution

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/7/text/en -->
FSAC-KK 34 is a posterior cervical fragment listed in the 2010 systematic account as Azhdarchidae gen. et sp. indet., without formal assignment to Alanqa saharica. Its preserved width across the projections is 83 mm. Comparison with Quetzalcoatlus material suggests a larger wingspan to the authors, not a measurement from complete wings of this species. The 2020 study notes another named Kem Kem taxon, Xericeps, so local postcranial remains cannot automatically be assigned to Alanqa.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Wingspan and jaw length depend on comparisons

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/text/en -->
The 2010 authors use a Quetzalcoatlus symphyseal proportion to estimate a mandibular length near 0.675 m for the type, depending on its original lower-jaw interpretation and borrowed proportions. The 2010 abstract and 2020 discussion give roughly 3–4 m wingspan for the type individual as a comparative estimate, not a measured complete span. The often cited approximately 6 m estimate concerns larger cervical material without formal species attribution and cannot establish a confirmed maximum for Alanqa. Reinterpreting the type as a rostrum does not itself recalculate jaw length or wingspan.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Relationships and possible synonymy need more material

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/text/en -->
The 2020 study discusses an earlier analysis placing Alanqa among thalassodromids and notes its assumption that the type was a mandibular symphysis; its own comparisons support Azhdarchidae. It also raises a possible relationship or synonymy with Xericeps, explicitly requiring more complete remains to demonstrate or reject synonymy. These are dated interpretations and hypotheses rather than decisions made here. Fixed PBDB identities, parent chains and occurrences are retained, with later classification literature still incomplete.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Read from type reinterpretation to specimen attribution

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/10/text/en -->
Start with the 2010/2020 interpretations of the type, then separate jaw fragments from the indeterminate cervical material before returning to locality and age records. In comparison with an associated skeleton, isolated jaw material depends more heavily on borrowed proportions; neither kind of material directly observes flight or feeding.
<!-- /evo:text -->
