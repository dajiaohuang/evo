## Small bones from a brief window
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/0/text/en -->
The Garbani Channel fauna in Montana preserves isolated ankle bones assigned to Purgatorius. Two named specimens, UCMP 197509 and UCMP 197517, are astragali and calcanei rather than parts of a skeleton found with teeth. Their late-Puercan setting places the sample near the beginning of the Paleocene, but the locality window is not a direct date on an animal.

## An ankle built for mobility
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/1/text/en -->
Comparisons of the joint surfaces and measurements place these bones near the mobile end of the mammal sample examined by the authors. The form supports an interpretation that the foot could invert and the animal may have used branches. This is a functional reading of ankle anatomy, not a direct observation of climbing or a complete reconstruction of the animal’s daily life.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Attribution is not ancestry
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerSections/2/text/en -->
The bones were attributed to Purgatorius through comparative size and anatomical analyses. Because they are isolated and lack associated teeth, the study does not demonstrate that they belong to a named dental species, establish membership in crown Primates, or identify a direct ancestor. The result is a useful early locomotor test with a deliberately narrow specimen base.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## atlas-profile / readerLimitations

<!-- evo:text /records/atlas-profile/readerLimitations/en -->
This is a specimen-focused guide to two isolated tarsals, not a complete species account. Their taxonomic attribution and locomotor meaning are comparative inferences; diet, population ecology and a direct place in primate ancestry are not established by this sample.
