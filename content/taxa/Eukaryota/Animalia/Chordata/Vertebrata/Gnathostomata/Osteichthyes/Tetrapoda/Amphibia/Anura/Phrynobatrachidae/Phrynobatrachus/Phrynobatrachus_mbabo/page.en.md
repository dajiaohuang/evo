COL26.8 accepts Phrynobatrachus mbabo Gvozdík, Necas, Dolinay, Zimkus, Schmitz & Fokam, 2020 as a species. Its recorded classification places it along Anura (order) → Phrynobatrachidae (family) → Phrynobatrachus (genus). The name and placement refer to the release issued on 20 August 2026; names in another checklist or fossil database require their own identity evidence.

## Two male sizes are distinct from five adult females
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq8rc
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
The two examined males measure 14.0 and 14.5 mm snout–vent length; five adult females measure 15.3–17.9 mm. A combined 14.0–17.9 mm diagnosis is not a male-only range. Central male throat asperities, rudimentary webbing and an inconspicuous tympanum resemble arcanus. The comparison adds relatively longer hind limbs and cranial differences, including a more gracile columella and shorter ventral squamosal ramus. Ratios and skeletal features concern examined material; neither combined size nor superficial resemblance identifies every frog, especially juvenile steindachneri collected in syntopy.

## Only the female holotype supplies live colour
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq8rc
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
The live female holotype has an orange-brown back with darker eyelids and interorbital region, white side spots, a light-yellow belly with a few chest spots and orange hind-limb undersides. Live colour was known only for this female; male and wider-series colour variation in the account comes from preserved specimens. The diagnostic description of female hind limbs as light orange and the holotype account as vivid orange are separate source descriptions, not an invented colour scale. No live male colour or toxin function is supplied.

## Three sequenced specimens share one short-fragment haplotype
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq8rc
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/4/text/en -->
All three sequenced mbabo specimens share one 510-base 16S haplotype. Distance to arcanus is 1.2% for that barcode and 5.0% across the longer 12S–16S fragment, with additional differences outside the barcode in a hypervariable region. Two inferred nuclear RAG1 haplotypes differ from arcanus. These are different marker comparisons and allelic units, not interchangeable distances or additional individual counts. The combined evidence supports the paper’s distinction while leaving range-wide genomic coverage and independent contemporary concept certification unproven.

## An attributed 1972 recording supplies the call account
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq8rc
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/5/text/en -->
The call analysis uses J.-L. Amiet’s 24 December 1972 recording, originally Phrynobatrachus sp. 5. Clicking note groups average 2.2 ± 0.3 s with 8–11 notes; notes average 14.8 ± 0.2 ms, repetition 4.8 ± 0.2 per second and dominant frequency 3.51 ± 0.05 kHz, all reported mean ± standard deviation. The last two notes are closer together, about 70 ms versus roughly 170 ms otherwise, and the final note lower pitched. Historical recording attribution is not a new monitored population; repeated notes do not establish frog count, and no recording temperature is invented.

## Bottle-laid eggs do not determine natural oviposition
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq8rc
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/6/text/en -->
The female holotype laid eggs overnight in a collecting bottle on 28 January 2000, in the middle of the local dry season. The paragraph gives no egg count, diameter, fertilization or hatching result. This capture event does not establish a natural egg substrate, full clutch-size range or species-wide breeding calendar. The source expressly says tadpoles are unknown. Unknown larvae cannot be replaced with arcanus larval traits or used to infer direct development, and the bottle observation supplies no parental-care evidence.

## Forest-night specimens and daytime meadow calls have different contexts
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/7/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq8rc
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/7/text/en -->
Examined specimens were active near a montane forest stream at about 20:00–21:30 and 20 °C. Other frogs were mixed with superficially similar juvenile steindachneri and not directly distinguished in the field. The cited older call account instead describes swampy meadows and daytime calling. The authors hypothesize that open grassland wetlands are typical and gallery forests visited less often; this is not an experimental habitat preference. Night captures and historical day calls are retained separately rather than turned into an exclusively diurnal or nocturnal rule.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## The type valley is confirmed; the wider ridge remains possible
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq8rc
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/8/text/en -->
The source confirms the Tchabal Mbabo type locality in Cameroon’s Adamawa region, about 2,000–2,100 m, with the female type label at 2,060 m. The historical Mayo Yim call locality is essentially the same Yim-stream valley. Occurrence along the roughly fifty-kilometre mountain ridge is suggested but not surveyed confirmation. The approximate locality interval is not a calculated physiological limit or complete mountain occupancy. Accessibility of the type valley helps explain sampling scope rather than establishing absence from less accessible ridge habitat.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Failure to find specimens in 2016 supports concern, not proved extinction
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/sourceIds/0 -->
primary-pmc7059761-rq8rc
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/9/text/en -->
The paper reports no mbabo found during a June 2016 survey and identifies 2000 as its latest specimen observation within the study evidence. It presumes severe decline by analogy with other highland Phrynobatrachus, and recommends Critically Endangered based on known occupancy below 10 km², one location and presumed decline. These are dated source observations and interpretation, not a current last-seen date, experimentally identified decline cause or proof of extinction. Local zero detections and a category recommendation do not provide a quantified population trajectory or a newly verified current IUCN assessment.
<!-- /evo:text -->
