# Vultur gryphus

## Ecuador distribution model

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/topic -->
distribution
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/0/text/en -->
A 2016 Ecuador study modeled 60 roosting locations from seven satellite-tagged condors with seven environmental predictors at 500 m resolution. It estimated an Ecuadorian extent of occurrence of 49,550 km² and area of occupancy of 14,106 km². These are country-level model outputs, not a global range map. The area follows the Results section; the abstract reports a different EOO of 49,725 km², an unresolved internal discrepancy.

## Dated census and modeled threats

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/topic -->
conservation
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/1/text/en -->
The same study estimated 94–102 condors (its Gaussian 90% confidence interval) in Ecuador from 93 birds counted during a synchronized two-day census in 2015. Its population-viability scenarios identified habitat loss as the greatest modeled threat in that country. The paper discusses an earlier national Critically Endangered listing; it is not a current IUCN or national reassessment.

## Nesting observations

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/topic -->
lifeHistory
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/2/text/en -->
The 2024 study compiled 22 egg-laying attempts at eight Ecuadorian nests during 2009–2021. Sixteen recorded nestlings stayed in the nest for six to ten months before first flight. A mean interval of 15 months between eggs came from one pair watched during 2011–2021; it is not a species-wide reproductive rate. Several records were opportunistic, so the study does not provide a uniform comparison among pairs.
<!-- /evo:text -->

## Nest-area interactions

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/topic -->
ecology
<!-- /evo:text -->

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/3/text/en -->
At Ecuadorian nest areas, researchers recorded encounters in which condors displaced other raptors and a Spectacled Bear. Systematic watches covered three sites during selected periods in 2017–2022. Lack of encounters at some nests could reflect lower observation effort. These observations describe nest-area defence; they do not measure the species-wide diet, scavenging network or frequency of interactions across its range.

## Relative weights of food categories

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/sections/15/text/en -->
The archive’s nonzero food weights are scavenged food and related discarded material 100%. Source code A indicates high compiler confidence in the source and diet information. These percentages standardize verbal descriptions rather than measure diet composition; zero does not prove an item is never eaten. ## Limitations

<!-- evo:text /records/catalogue-profile/limitations/en -->
This is a guide to historical AVONET v7 data. Morphological values are species averages from the listed samples, not identification diagnoses or ranges of individual variation. Mass uses separately cited literature or weighing sources; morphological sample counts must not be assigned to mass. Habitat, diet and migration are coarse categories; geography concerns historical breeding and resident ranges only. One-to-one historical crosswalks and a matching catalogue name provide the association here, not proof of equivalent species concepts after later splits or lumps. Reproduction, current populations and conservation status require other sources.
This guide combines a 2016 Ecuador model and census with a 2024 paper on geographically and temporally limited nests.
