## What connects a larval sea squirt to a vertebrate? {#reading-introduction}
A swimming sea-squirt larva, an amphioxus and a vertebrate look very different. Their chordate structures become easier to compare when we follow where those structures develop—and what happens to a sea squirt’s tail after attachment. Genome comparisons offer a separate test of how the living branches are related.

## Three living groups, one phylum {#living-lineages}
Chordata is represented today by lancelets (Cephalochordata), tunicates (called urochordates in the 2008 genome paper), and vertebrates. A comparative genome study used the Florida lancelet *Branchiostoma floridae* alongside sampled tunicates and vertebrates; a separate anatomical study followed the tunicate *Ciona robusta* (called *C. intestinalis* type A in that paper) from larva to juvenile. These names identify broad lineages; their classification alone does not state which branch split first.

## Structures that mark the chordate body plan {#shared-structures}
A comparative account of amphioxus, tunicates, and vertebrates discusses a notochord, a hollow dorsal neural tube, a perforated pharynx, segmented trunk muscles, and a post-anal tail among their shared anatomical and developmental features. The notochord is an internal rod along the body axis; the hollow neural tube lies on its dorsal side, and “post-anal” means that the tail extends beyond the anal opening. This is a developmental body-plan pattern, not a promise that every adult visibly retains the complete set.

## Ciona changes its chordate form at metamorphosis {#ciona-life-stages}
In the staged anatomy study of *Ciona robusta* (*C. intestinalis* type A), the swimming larva has a tail containing a notochord, nerve cord, and muscles. After attachment, the tail is absorbed: the study places this period around 27–30 hours post-fertilization at 18 °C and reports that absorption usually takes 75–90 minutes at 18–20 °C, with duration varying by the extent of regression. The post-metamorphic juvenile shows pharyngeal fissures and an endostyle after the larval body plan is no longer recognizable. These observations concern one tunicate developmental series, not every chordate adult.

## A tree is a study result {#tree-in-the-study}
The local COL26.8 record identifies Chordata as an accepted phylum; it does not encode a branching order. Putnam and colleagues’ 2008 genome study analyzed 1,090 orthologous genes with Bayesian and maximum-likelihood methods, using the Florida lancelet genome and sampled tunicates, including *Ciona intestinalis* and *Oikopleura dioica*. Their analyses placed cephalochordates as the earliest-diverging living chordate lineage and tunicates as the sister group of vertebrates. The paper contrasts this with a preliminary 146-locus result that weakly grouped amphioxus with echinoderms. Those are study-specific phylogenetic results, not changes to the classification record; the comparison leaves taxon sampling and character choice central to the question.
