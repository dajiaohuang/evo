## atlas-profile / overview {#s-a9fcc9060e}
COL26.8 accepts Macaca silenus (Linnaeus, 1758). The ecological observations below come from specific groups, places and periods, while genetic studies use mitochondrial samples; these records do not form a complete range-wide account.

## atlas-profile / ecology / diet {#s-72c4c3b9f1}
At Puthuthottam, one troop ate plant parts from 19 species and other foods during a 2016 dry-season study. Plant feeding was greater in forest interior than at the edge; invertebrate feeding was greater in an open patch than in the interior.

## atlas-profile / ecology / habitat {#s-6ab4494894}
Over 480 follow-hours in one habituated troop covered forest interior, edge, an open patch and a settlement from February to May 2016. Active foraging was higher in the open patch and edge, and lower in the settlement than in the interior.

## atlas-profile / evidenceSummary {#s-53660e8b50}
Local feeding and activity observations cover one troop in one dry season. Genetic samples, a separate four-group seed-dispersal preprint and the checklist answer different questions; together they do not provide a range-wide or annual ecological synthesis.

## How field observations and genetic samples answer different questions {#s-9a39688794}
Forest interiors, edges and open patches provide different feeding settings in the Puthuthottam study. One troop was followed during the dry season, while genetic studies compared mitochondrial samples from Western Ghats sites. These records describe local behaviour and sampled lineages, not a complete account of the species.

## Sampling across the Western Ghats {#s-c0db3bd0c1}
A 2010–2012 genetic study sampled wild animals at 15 sites in Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu, and included separately identified captive blood samples. These are study locations, not the full present-day range.

## Activity in a forest-fragment landscape {#s-74592b3bef}
From February to May 2016, researchers followed one habituated troop at Puthuthottam for more than 480 hours across forest interior, edge, an open patch and a settlement. Active foraging was higher in the open patch and at the edge, and lower in the settlement than in the interior. The comparison concerns one troop in one dry season.

## Foods recorded in that troop {#s-99d08ed3db}
The troop ate parts from 19 plant species as well as non-plant foods. In the habitat comparisons, invertebrate feeding was more frequent in the open patch than in forest interior, while plant feeding was greater in the interior than at the edge. This is not a complete species diet.

## A four-group seed-dispersal preprint {#s-ebe027c0a3}
A version-one 2024 bioRxiv preprint reports 375.9 hours of focal observations across four groups in a 92-hectare Puduthottam fragment. Subadults consumed a greater variety of native and non-native fruits than adults, and dispersed fewer Ficus seeds than adult females. The sampling year is not reported; this result is from a preprint.

## Mitochondrial lineages around the Palghat Gap {#s-e1a4cf3b6d}
A Western Ghats study distinguished sampled mitochondrial lineages north and south of the Palghat Gap. A fossil-calibrated model using 893 mitochondrial bases estimated their mean most-recent common ancestor at 2.11 million years ago. This is not a nuclear-genome species tree or a direct fossil date for the species’ origin.

## Genetic diversity in forest fragments {#s-a6193aa7e0}
A separate study compared HVR-I mitochondrial samples from eight Anamalai forest fragments with nearby continuous-forest samples and found lower diversity in the fragments. It describes those samples and markers, not population viability or extinction risk.

## What these studies do not measure {#s-1f2d77fc61}
The selected sources do not synthesize body size, life history, a complete range, annual ecology or current formal conservation status. Feeding observations come from one troop and season; the seed-dispersal results remain a preprint.
