## Brachycephalus crispus: bones within rough skin {#primary-intro}
This orange frog has small bones embedded in its rough skin. The original study combined skeletal examination with observations in forest leaf litter at Cunha, Brazil. What distinguishes these scattered skin bones from a continuous bony shield?

## Skin bones without a dorsal shield {#primary-body}
Cleared and stained specimens showed osteoderms—small bones within the skin—scattered across the back. Unlike B. ephippium, the described material lacks a dorsal bony shield. Table 1 separates males (n = 22; snout–vent length 11.5–12.9 mm), females (n = 25; 11.9–15.6 mm), and juveniles (n = 6; 9.5–11.3 mm). Snout–vent length measures from the snout to the cloacal opening; the smallest value belongs to juveniles.

## Daytime activity in leaf litter {#primary-habitat}
At the studied locality, frogs were active by day in forest leaf litter at approximately 800–1100 m elevation. Males were usually heard beneath leaves, although exposed calling was also observed. These are observations at Cunha, rather than evidence of a species-wide density.

## An anatomical comparison {#primary-comparison}
The authors distinguished B. crispus from several congeners using rough skin, white spotting and the distribution of bone growth. This diagnostic comparison does not establish a sister-species relationship.

## Examining the skin and skeleton {#primary-study}
The 2014 description examined stained skeletons and scanning electron micrographs. Bone ornamentation was weaker in some juveniles than in adults, showing why life stage matters when comparing this rough-skinned frog.

## Name and placement in COL26.8 {#s-fe8018a1f7}
In its 20 August 2026 release, COL26.8 lists Brachycephalus crispus Condez, Clemente-Carvalho, Haddad & Reis, 2014 as a species in Anura, family Brachycephalidae, genus Brachycephalus.
