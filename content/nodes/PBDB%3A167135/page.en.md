## One holotype, three interpretations of its jaw {#s-0ed3b7d26a}
FSAC-KK 26 is the same type specimen in all three accounts. The 2010 description reads it as a lower jaw; a 2020 study reinterprets it as an upper-jaw rostrum; Smith and colleagues tentatively return to a lower-jaw reading in 2023. They distinguish its paired rear projections, which do not meet at the front, from the single central boss on FSAC-KK 5204 and 5205, which they identify as upper jaws. Cross-sections, foramina and the angle of the sides all contribute to the comparison. These are changing interpretations of one specimen, not three type fossils.

## Jaw fragments and isolated body bones have different certainty {#s-8bdd17689e}
The 2023 review expands the comparison of jaws attributed to Alanqa and revisits earlier referrals. It calls FSAC-KK 28 an indeterminate Azhdarchoidea jaw of morphotype A. Three neck vertebrae once referred to Alanqa—CMN 50801, LINHM 014 and FSAC-KK 5077—remain indeterminate azhdarchids in that discussion; humerus CMN 50814 is indeterminate Azhdarchoidea. Without bones found in association, these body fragments cannot be securely attached to a genus or species named from jaws. UOP-PAL KK 0006 and FSAC-KK 5213 are two 3D printed models of one privately held fossil, not two animals.

## A split ridge changes how jaws are compared {#s-4ccee4c237}
The 2023 authors argue that the type’s two rear projections remain separate toward the front. That challenges comparisons which treated them as one V-shaped ridge. The study keeps Alanqa in Azhdarchidae, while reviewing how earlier phylogenetic analyses interpreted the type as either an upper or lower jaw. The 2020 and 2023 accounts differ in their description of the specimen identity used in one earlier analysis, so the published summaries do not by themselves settle how its matrix coded the character. The jaw comparison informs classification, but does not settle the family tree.

## The same type was read as a jaw and a rostrum {#s-3182defbb4}
The holotype, FSAC-KK 26, is housed at the Faculté des Sciences Ain Chock, Université Hassan II, in Casablanca, Morocco. The 2010 naming paper describes it as an almost complete lower-jaw symphysis; the 2020 Kem Kem study reads the same fossil as a rostrum and reports that it was collected in place at Aferdou N’Chaft in 2008. The jaw identity changed between papers; the specimen did not.

## Three fragments preserve only part of the jaw {#s-9dff8d8ee9}
The 2010 description records three adjoining orange-brown fragments with 344 mm of preserved length. The front tip is missing, as is part of the underside of the middle fragment. Extending the margins and comparing related jaws led the authors to estimate that as much as 80 mm of the tip may be absent; this is a reconstruction, not measured fossil. The fragments retain three-dimensional shape and thin bone walls, but they are not a complete skull or an associated skeleton.

## Toothless margins and a disputed central boss {#s-312d3b0ece}
The 2010 authors called Alanqa toothless because five jaws assigned to it lack tooth sockets. They described straight jaw edges, a slender outline and a cross-section that changes from nearly triangular to Y-shaped. They first interpreted the type’s rear prominence as part of a V-shaped lower-jaw structure. Under the 2020 upper-jaw interpretation, it becomes a boss on the palate, compared with surfaces that met it on the lower jaw. Foramina and grooves suggest how blood vessels and nerves may have reached a beak sheath, but they do not preserve the whole sheath or reveal the animal’s diet.

## Other referred fragments come from separate places {#s-4b9112edba}
The 2010 paper also refers BSP 1996 I 36, FSAC-KK 31, BSP 1993 IX 338 and FSAC-KK 27 to the species. FSAC-KK 31 came from Boumerade and FSAC-KK 27 from Aferdou N’Chaft; the BSP fossils have only the tentative provenance “possibly near Taouz.” The referrals rely on comparisons of shape and size. In one figure, the type is paired with a separate rostrum to reconstruct a jaw, not because the pieces are known to come from one animal. Their fit also depends on which way the type is interpreted.

## A neck vertebra remains unassigned to species {#s-d798a1cf0b}
FSAC-KK 34 is a rear neck-vertebra fragment that the 2010 account lists as an indeterminate azhdarchid, not as Alanqa saharica. It is 83 mm wide across its projections. Comparing it with Quetzalcoatlus led the authors to suggest a large wingspan, but no complete Alanqa wings are present to measure. The 2020 study also names another Kem Kem pterosaur, Xericeps, so a loose neck bone from the region cannot automatically be assigned to Alanqa.

## Size estimates borrow proportions from other pterosaurs {#s-d691ea1c84}
The 2010 authors estimated a lower-jaw length of about 0.675 m by scaling from Quetzalcoatlus. That estimate depends on reading the type as a lower jaw and borrowing another animal’s proportions. The 2010 abstract and 2020 discussion give an estimated 3–4 m wingspan for the type individual, also by comparison rather than direct measurement of complete wings. A frequently repeated estimate of about 6 m concerns larger neck bones not formally assigned to this species, so it is not a confirmed maximum for Alanqa. Reading the type as an upper jaw does not itself recalculate either estimate.

## Azhdarchid placement and possible synonymy remain debated {#s-655e36132c}
The 2020 study reviews an earlier analysis that placed Alanqa among thalassodromids while treating the type as a lower-jaw symphysis. Its own comparisons support Azhdarchidae. The same study raises a possible relationship—or even synonymy—with Xericeps, but says more complete fossils are needed to test it. These remain interpretations and hypotheses attributed to the cited studies, not a settled classification.

## Follow the type from anatomy to locality {#s-1181aa59cf}
The type’s changing jaw interpretation helps make sense of the other jaw fragments. The neck material is a separate set of remains and includes pieces not assigned to species. The locality and age records then show where the database places these fossils. Neither isolated jaws nor an unattached neck bone directly records flight or feeding.

## Evidence limits {#s-e717f658a1}
The account summarizes selected sections from studies published in 2010, 2020 and 2023, not a complete monograph or inspection of every specimen, image, supplement or later analysis. Those sources disagree about whether the type is an upper or lower jaw, how one earlier phylogenetic analysis treated it, and the reported horizon of the Xericeps type. The 2023 jaw interpretation is tentative, and the possible relationship or synonymy with Xericeps remains unresolved. Database records organize selected occurrences but do not settle these anatomical or taxonomic questions.
