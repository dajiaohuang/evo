## One skeleton preserved across four blocks {#s-cea5974da9}
The holotype, NMS G.2021.6.1–4, comes from the Upper Lonfearn Member of the Lealt Shale Formation on Scotland’s Isle of Skye. The 2025 study describes one associated skeleton divided among a main slab, a skull block, a ventral counterslab and a separate wing-phalanx block. It preserves delicate palate, hyoid and braincase bones, a complete neck series and a particularly clear right hand. The back vertebrae and ribs are scattered; the pelvis, tail and hindlimbs are poorly preserved. Hard limestone still hides many surfaces, even where the bones retain three-dimensional form.

## CT reveals cavities; muscles must be inferred {#s-1ef6132e26}
Micro-CT scans of the detached skull block reveal internal spaces that were then outlined by hand. Bone and surrounding rock contrast clearly in the snout but less clearly at the rear of the skull, so those boundaries are harder to trace; the brain and inner-ear cavities are more distinct. Muscle positions come from a separate step: the authors compare ridges, hollows and surface textures with crocodilians and birds. The cavities are visible in the scan, but the muscle arrangement is a reconstruction, not preserved tissue or a record of flight.

## Tooth wear leaves more than one feeding possibility {#s-f37ffbcae1}
Dearc has longer, recurved teeth at the front of the jaws and shorter, straight, prism-shaped teeth behind them. Erosion has removed much of some front teeth, leaving roots or middle sections; several rear crowns are chipped, cracked or flaked. By comparing the teeth with Dorygnathus and other pterosaurs, the authors suggest Dearc could handle relatively hard prey. They also propose that the overlapping front teeth may have helped seize fish. No meal is preserved with the skeleton, so neither idea fixes its diet or the share of fish it ate.

## The sternum and upper-arm crests help identify Dearc {#s-b1865cef55}
The 2025 study identifies two proposed distinguishing features: a deltopectoral crest that widens toward the shoulder and rises above the ulnar crest, and a sternum whose front edge projects forward. The sternum is broad and thin; its rear may not have finished ossifying. Few other pterosaur sterna are well enough preserved for a strong comparison. The two upper arms are exposed differently and show local flattening and fractures, so their differing curves do not establish asymmetry in life. These bone shapes help with identification and attachment comparisons, but do not measure muscle size or wing power.

## Bone landmarks suggest a possible flight-muscle pattern {#s-0285423cd0}
The authors link the enlarged deltopectoral crest to the pectoralis muscle and compare other marks on the upper arm with attachment sites for latissimus dorsi and coracobrachialis. From this pattern they propose that Dearc may have drawn its upper arm inward and forward during flight. Differences from Rhamphorhynchus suggest that similarly sized pterosaurs need not have moved their wings in the same way. This remains a working hypothesis: the shoulder girdle and hand bones are incomplete, and body size, shared ancestry and convergent evolution are hard to separate. A future muscle-and-skeleton model could test the proposal.

## Curved hand claws may have helped with grasping {#s-097c574b87}
The right hand preserves digits I–III; only part of the elongated fourth, wing-supporting digit remains. The terminal claw bones have strong flexor attachment areas and associated sesamoid bones. Their curvature, compared in two dimensions with that of climbing and perching birds, leads the authors to suggest a role in grasping or climbing, perhaps with an additional role in catching prey. A claw bone is only the bony core of a keratin-covered claw, and bird comparisons cannot show what Dearc did. How often it climbed, what it might have climbed on, and whether its claws caught prey remain unknown. Because the study’s reported lengths conflict with its later ranking of the third claw as longest, this page does not rank them by size.

## The published family placements differ {#s-577e70c710}
The 2025 paper uses the Angustinaripterini grouping introduced in the 2022 description. It also notes a different analysis, which includes Skiphosoura, placing Dearc nearer to Monofenestrata. Its relatively long neck and other traits could reflect different body regions evolving at different rates or convergence linked to size. Calling Dearc “transitional” does not make it a demonstrated ancestor. The database’s accepted-name hierarchy is a record-keeping path; these competing family-level placements remain attributed to their respective studies.

## One eligible occurrence links to one collection {#s-6fd99363de}
The fixed Paleobiology Database (PBDB) pterosaur roster accepts Dearc sgiathanach beneath the database parent Dearc. This guide draws on one eligible species-level occurrence, linked to one collection and one reference identifier. These are counts of database records, collections and citations—not counts of bones, specimens or individual animals.

## The collection is from Scotland’s Lealt Shale {#s-f41bfcba4f}
The selected collection is named Rubha nam Brathairean pterosaur (Scotland), with region code UK and stratum Lealt Shale. Its database age bracket is 168.2–165.3 Ma, with the early bound assigned to the Middle Bathonian. The collection and rock-unit names follow the database wording. One recorded locality cannot give a complete map of the species or show that it was absent elsewhere.

## A bracket around records, not a continuous timeline {#s-8d2cdf8c3f}
The selected occurrence falls within an outer bracket of 168.2–165.3 Ma (Ma means million years ago). This combines the earliest and latest database bounds; it does not mean the animal is recorded continuously throughout that span, or date its origin, extinction or lifespan. Each collection keeps its own bracket. The broad age range is not reduced to a more precise specimen date.

## The collection setting is not the animal’s habitat {#s-7d96f25129}
The Rubha nam Brathairean collection carries the database environment label “lagoonal” and preservation-mode label “body.” These labels describe the collection and its depositional setting, not Dearc’s daily habitat or diet. A collection-level label also does not describe every fossil in it. Where the database has no value, none is supplied here.

## The database citation and the anatomy study have different roles {#s-9b68b1fd1a}
The database collection cites Jagielska et al. (2022), reference 81241. The anatomical details summarized above come from a separate 2025 osteological study, listed with its eight authors, publication date and open license. The two citations therefore support different parts of this account: one is attached to the collection record, while the other describes the holotype’s anatomy.

## Evidence limits {#s-9af24a3560}
The anatomical account here summarizes selected sections of the 2025 study rather than a complete monograph. It does not cover every structure or original image, nor the full 2022 description, including its wingspan regressions and histology. Muscle use, feeding and climbing are interpretations drawn from bones, teeth and comparisons; they are not direct records of behavior. This material also does not establish Dearc’s life history or settle its placement among pterosaurs.
