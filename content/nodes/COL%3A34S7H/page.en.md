## Triangulum was associated with clade H rather than clade C {#s-13cbb72c0d}
The revision assigned the name triangulum to clade H using the holotype’s large triangular mark and the broadly stated Brazilian type locality. The original holotype was 25 mm snout–vent length and of unknown sex, so it is separate from the later adult male sample range. Many earlier clade C records had been called triangulum; the revision instead associated clade C with reticulatus based on its type and small rounded marks. This nomenclatural review does not certify every old triangulum label, and the paper’s own uncertainty over Nacebe material is retained rather than automatically converting that historical record into a confirmed new locality.

## An hourglass pattern can be incomplete {#s-a7a97d8815}
The diagnosis sampled twenty-six males at 28.6–34.4 mm snout–vent length and five females at 37.5–41.9 mm. It described a white or live bright-yellow dorsum with a dark-brown hourglass mark, sometimes only half present, and variable rounded spots or ovoid bands on limbs; some individuals were reticulated. The axillary membrane, basal finger webbing, toe webbing and single palmar tubercle form part of the combination. Live photographic accounts give yellowish-orange limb undersides and webbing at night and pale orange by day, with a bronze iris. These variations preclude identification from a complete hourglass alone or assuming that a missing half invalidates an otherwise matching specimen.

## Longer advertisement calls with secondary notes {#s-1027da9602}
Advertisement calls contained a Type I trill averaging 0.15 s and thirteen to twenty-one pulses, followed by one to three shorter Type II notes. Whole calls averaged 0.26 s, ranged 0.22–0.36 s and had mean dominant frequency about 2,456 Hz. The aggressive-call account described four to five Type II notes, shorter inter-note gaps, mean whole duration 0.24 s and dominant frequency about 2,412 Hz. These are separate source classifications, not pooled note distributions. Several recordings could contribute to an individual average under the study’s methods; summary denominators therefore should not be invented as numbers of independent populations. The measurements do not quantify mate choice or prove reproductive isolation from related species.

## Amazonian records and a questioned Bolivian report {#s-c76ea0ab6f}
The revision’s confirmed distribution account included Ecuador, Peru and Brazil, with known locality elevations 34–387 m. It questioned a Nacebe, Bolivia report because the described specimens resembled reticulatus; that is a suggested identity rather than a new independently checked occurrence. Frogs were found at night near secondary-forest pools, lakes and swamps, on vegetation 100–420 cm above ground. Regional vegetation descriptions mention much taller forest canopies, which are not frog perch heights. These observations do not establish continuous occupancy, nesting sites or a measured preference for secondary forest over intact habitat.

## A historical range polygon is not occupied habitat {#s-b7d265cf9b}
The study estimated a minimum-convex distribution polygon of 923,514 square kilometres for this account. It reported 52,604 square kilometres (5.7%) degraded by human activities. Natural-vegetation estimates used the Amazon Deforestation 2010 layer. A polygon includes intervening space and is not equivalent to occupied area, population size or measured percentage population loss. The paper proposed Least Concern; this dated recommendation is not independent verification of a current Red List assessment. Protection and broad range alone do not demonstrate a stable population or current site security.
