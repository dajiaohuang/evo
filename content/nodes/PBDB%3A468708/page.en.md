## An articulated skeleton with a nearly complete skull {#s-c85b35f60b}
The 2022 description names Natovenator polydontus from holotype MPC-D 102/114, a mostly articulated skeleton with a nearly complete skull. It comes from the Upper Cretaceous Baruungoyot Formation at Hermiin Tsav in southern Mongolia and is housed at the Institute of Paleontology in Ulaanbaatar. Compression has displaced the front of the skull slightly, and the forelimbs are only partly exposed. The authors used micro-CT on the skull and partial sacrum to examine internal anatomy; scan reconstructions do not supply missing bones.

## What the numerous teeth and long neck preserve {#s-b1ebf1489e}
Each premaxilla carries 13 teeth, larger than the three markedly reduced front maxillary teeth. Despite poor preservation at the back, at least 23 alveoli are identifiable in each maxilla and dentary. Maxillary and dentary crowns lack serrations; large replacement premaxillary teeth underpin an interpretation of delayed replacement. The neck includes ten elongated cervicals, with most of the fifth missing. Teeth and neck anatomy support the authors’ prey-capture hypothesis without directly identifying diet, prey species or feeding frequency.

## Backward-directed ribs inform the body reconstruction {#s-b0111d8ce4}
Twelve dorsals are preserved. Ribs articulated with the second to seventh dorsals point posterolaterally, with nearly horizontal, broadly arched proximal shafts. The authors reconstruct a flattened, elongated ribcage. Table 1 measures proximal rib angles against the vertebral column in ventral view: the third rib is 40° in this specimen, 60–70° in the compared diving birds, 80° in Shri devi and 134° in the ostrich. These positional anatomical comparisons do not measure swimming speed or drag or define a universal aquatic threshold. Missing ribs have no measurement. Figure 4 marks missing parts of the skeletal reconstruction in dark grey; it is not a complete fossil.

## How swimming and fish eating are hypothesized {#s-31eb705d18}
The paper combines ribcage shape, neck and snout anatomy to propose a semiaquatic, potentially capable swimming predator. The genus name’s swimming-hunter meaning also refers to that hypothesis. The authors explicitly leave aquatic locomotion unknown; likely forelimb propulsion is inferred through comparison and relationship with Halszkaraptor. Retracted nares remain a debated aquatic indicator, and delayed tooth replacement does not directly establish aquatic habits. The study does not establish behavior through measured drag, swimming speed or direct food remains. Figure 5 illustrates proposed swimming behavior.
