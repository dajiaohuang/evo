## Conservation evidence {#s-393f707d40}
How many golden lion tamarins were estimated in 2014? Researchers used a point-transect survey with lures across seven management units and estimated 3,706 animals (95% confidence interval: 1,863–5,383). They estimated that about 59% came from remnant wild populations, 34% were descendants of captive-born animals later released, and 7% were descendants of wild groups moved to new sites. These are survey estimates for 2014, not direct counts or current abundance.

## Evidence limits {#s-ab5e2609b5}
This seven-unit survey describes estimated abundance and population origins in 2014; it does not establish current numbers or later trends.

## The name in the historical life-history record {#s-f57aa52aca}
COL26.8 lists *Leontopithecus rosalia* as an accepted species in Callitrichidae. The life-history values below come from the same-name entry in Ernest’s 2003 table, whose taxonomy followed Wilson and Reeder (1993). A matching name does not resolve later taxonomic splits and merges.

## Gestation and first reproduction {#s-268fc49a8b}
How long is pregnancy, and when do females first breed? Ernest’s compilation gives an average active gestation of 4.35 months and a female age at first reproduction of 25.01 months. Active gestation covers fetal development but excludes delayed implantation. The source defines first reproduction as the female’s age at first breeding when conception is possible; when a source reports age at first birth, the compilation subtracts gestation. The value is not sexual-maturity age or age at first birth. Both figures summarize sources rather than set a timetable for every female.

## From newborn offspring to weaning {#s-46d265691a}
The compiled averages are 55.07 g at birth, 4.3 months to milk independence, and 165 g at that transition. Newborn mass refers to one offspring; the source excludes records explicitly measured more than 12 hours after birth. Milk independence means an infant no longer depends on milk; its age is counted from birth and the mass refers to that transition. The sources do not consistently separate wild and captive conditions or localities, so these averages are not a growth curve or an account of parental care.

## Litter size and frequency {#s-274b348ea1}
How often did females give birth in the compilation? Average litter size was 1.95 offspring, and the average was 1.75 litters per female per year. Litter size counts young born, not embryo counts or reproductive scars; the fraction is an average, not a literal litter. Litter frequency counts births per female over 12 months. The compiler excluded captive studies designed to maximize production when possible, but not every record is confirmed as wild. Multiplying the two averages would not give an individual female’s yearly reproductive success.

## How to read maximum recorded lifespan {#s-9cbc78263d}
The longest entry in the historical table is 338 months. Unlike the averaged traits, this field keeps the longest male or female record and replaces it when a longer one is found. It is not average lifespan, a wild survival curve or the life expectancy of a typical tamarin. The row does not specify all conditions of that record.

## Limited fields and uninterpreted content {#s-14b90b7789}
The historical table has a positive value for each of eight interpreted life-history fields; adult mass is excluded. A filled row does not make the species’ biology complete. Missing entries do not mean the animal lacks a trait, and relatives or other tables are not used to fill them. These data do not establish a complete account of diet, behavior, distribution or conservation.

## Limits of these historical data {#s-c04a44685e}
How far can these numbers be compared? This is a historical life-history record from Ernest (2003), not a species monograph. Its taxonomy follows Wilson and Reeder (1993); a separate PanTHERIA same-name table links its MSW93 and MSW05 names, but that does not prove full equivalence with the current COL26.8 concept. Except for lifespan, the compilation generally averages literature values. Inputs may be individual observations, published averages or range midpoints when data are sparse. Each source has equal weight regardless of sample size, and the same underlying studies may recur. The summaries therefore are not local population estimates or timetables for every individual. Adult mass remains in the source table but is not interpreted here. The underlying species entries in the cited literature were not individually read, so no current conservation claim is inferred.
