## Several skulls contribute palatal evidence {#s-8574f768e6}
The 2020 study redescribes earlier and later-collected skull material, primarily using IVPP V 4063 for its well-preserved palate, with V 4064, V 4065 and additional skull or palatal fragments for comparison. V 4064 preserves an articulated skull and mandible; other material has different damage or compression. The combined description draws on several specimens rather than a complete skeleton belonging to one individual.

## Upturned toothless tips and an expanded tooth-bearing region {#s-675e497e9d}
The skull has a prominent sagittal crest and relatively small, high-set orbit. The upturned anterior upper and lower jaw tips are toothless. A posterior maxillary expansion bears four or five teeth with broad, roughly oval bases. Most tabulated skulls have fourteen or fifteen upper teeth per side, but V 4065 has thirteen on the right. Observed variation should not become a fixed species-wide count, and toothless tips do not make the entire jaw edentulous.

## Wear and bone shape support a hard-food hypothesis {#s-7b7f8d1693}
Smooth tooth wear surfaces in V 26256, together with robust teeth, toothless tips and a possible beak covering, support the 2020 authors’ hard-food interpretation. Probing or dislodging shellfish or hard-shelled insects with pincer-like tips comes from earlier comparative hypotheses rather than a preserved feeding event. It does not establish a fixed menu, obligate shellfish diet, measured bite force or common ecology across all collections.

## Corresponding jaw grooves may record a horny sheath {#s-0f12d72b40}
Corresponding grooves run along the outer upper and lower jaws. Some upper grooves lie near expected sutures, whereas the anterior lower groove cannot be interpreted in the same way. The authors therefore propose impressions of a horny covering’s edge, also considering crest-base striae and small foramina. These bone-surface clues do not directly preserve a complete sheath or establish its thickness, color or entire boundary.

## CT reveals sutures obscured at the surface {#s-4e94335e20}
The 2024 study scans V 4063, V 26257 and V 26258. Irregular interlocking boundaries inside thick palatal elements distinguish sutures from fractures, and segmented reconstructions identify contacts. This page reports the paper’s findings without inspecting raw slices, operating segmentation models or independently identifying sutures. Three scanned specimens do not cover every known specimen of the species.

## A Y-shaped palatine connects bones and openings {#s-0c326ba4c4}
In the 2024 reconstruction, two dorsoventrally flat rami form a Y-shaped palatine. The anterior ramus contacts the maxilla’s palatine process. The lateral ramus reaches the outer palate, thins medially and contacts the pterygoid dorsally. The opposing palatines meet posteriorly to form the choanal rear margin. A small palatine foramen at the pterygoid contact is hidden ventrally and visible from behind and above. This Y shape belongs to the palatine rather than the outline of the lower jaw.

## Part of the former lateral pterygoid process becomes ectopterygoid {#s-f9c0c7a50d}
The 2020 description emphasizes a two-part lateral pterygoid process. The 2024 CT reconstruction reinterprets the structure previously given that name as the ectopterygoid: an inward flat curved portion with foramina and a slender outer curved bar contacting the jugal and separating the postpalatine and subtemporal openings. Shape observations and revised element identity must be traced separately; the new name does not represent an extra bone.

## Opening names change with revised bone boundaries {#s-c788c3b24c}
The 2024 paper calls the most anterior opening lateral to the choana the maxillopalatine fenestra because the maxilla borders it anteriorly and the palatine posteriorly; some earlier descriptions called it the postpalatine fenestra. Subsequent opening names are adjusted accordingly, and the species’ small pterygoid foramen is distinguished from larger pterygoid fenestrae in other taxa. Position and bounding bones must be checked before comparing opening counts by name.

## Skull fusion provides limited maturity evidence {#s-4651b5d66e}
The 2020 authors interpret V 4063 as at least a subadult because its skull elements are strongly fused, while reporting earlier adult and relative-maturity interpretations for other specimens. These do not establish age in years, reproductive status or a complete growth curve. A thinner rostrum in V 26256 was proposed as possibly sex-related, but without independent sex evidence here it is not identified as a confirmed female.

## Regional association does not establish same-horizon coexistence {#s-9887da310e}
The study places its material in the Early Cretaceous Tugulu Group of Urho–Delunshan, Xinjiang, and discusses material from the eastern Junggar Basin. Records of Dsungaripterus and Noripterus at the same localities suggest possible coexistence, but the 2020 authors explicitly call for fieldwork to establish whether they occur in the same horizon. The three selected database collections are not matched individually to scanned specimens and do not establish competition, a shared menu or simultaneous death.

## Compare revised bone boundaries with Caupedactylus {#s-5bed25aebb}
[Caupedactylus ybaka](../../../Neoazhdarchia/Tapejaroidea/Tapejaridae/Tapejarinae/Caupedactylus/Caupedactylus_ybaka/page.en.md) contributes to both papers’ palatal comparisons. Read the newly scanned Dsungaripterus material first, then Caupedactylus comparisons based on earlier illustrations, and compare pterygoid openings and palatine identities. Similarities in 2020 support the authors’ cited relationship interpretation, while 2024 revises bone boundaries. No matrix was rerun here, and palatal resemblance is not presented as an established sister relationship.
