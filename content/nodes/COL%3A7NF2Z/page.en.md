## A swimming larva, then a different body {#opening}
What changes when a tunicate larva stops swimming? In the ascidian *Ciona robusta* (called *Ciona intestinalis* type A in the same study), Hotta and colleagues followed a mobile larva through adhesion and tail absorption as juvenile anatomy appeared. This is one documented ascidian life history, not a template for every tunicate.

## Chordate structures in the Ciona tail {#body}
In the early swimming larva of *Ciona robusta*, the tail contains the notochord, a flexible supporting rod, the nerve cord, muscles, and an endodermal strand. The trunk holds sensory structures including the sensory vesicle, ocellus, and otolith. These are anatomical observations from *Ciona*, one ascidian representative; they are not claims that every tunicate keeps the same adult form.

## Attachment, tail absorption, and body-axis rotation {#life}
At 18 °C, the studied swimming-larva period runs from 17.5 to 24 hours after fertilization. Adhesion follows at 24–27 hours; tail absorption begins at 27–30 hours after fertilization and usually finishes in 75–90 minutes at 18–20 °C. After the tail is absorbed, the inner organs rotate through about 90° as the juvenile body axis forms. These times describe the study conditions, not a universal clock for Tunicata.

Tunicata also contains different lifestyles. Delsuc and colleagues describe Thaliacea—salps, doliolids, and pyrosomes—as pelagic, with solitary and colonial phases; the same passage contrasts them with mostly solitary, sessile close relatives. This is a lineage-level contrast, not a habitat claim for every species.

## A tunicate tree is an inference from sampled genes {#relationships}
Delsuc and colleagues analyzed 258 nuclear orthologous genes across 63 deuterostome taxa, including 18 tunicates but only one cephalochordate. Their best-fitting site-heterogeneous model recovered tunicates and vertebrates as the two branches of Olfactores, excluding cephalochordates, and placed *Oikopleura dioica* as sister to the other sampled tunicates. They cautioned that the unusually long *Oikopleura* branch could still create a long-branch-attraction artifact, so that placement was not conclusive. 

The same tree placed Thaliacea as sister to Phlebobranchia plus Aplousobranchia and recovered the traditional class Ascidiacea as paraphyletic. These are hypotheses from this dataset, not direct observations of ancestry.



Their relaxed-clock model estimated the earliest split between Appendicularia and other tunicates at 447 ± 20 million years ago (mean age ± standard deviation), with a 95% credibility interval of 484–411 million years. The authors described these dates as tentative: they had no reliable fossil calibration within Tunicata, and the estimated intervals were broad.

## What the studies actually examined {#research}
Hotta and colleagues combined time-lapse movies, confocal microscopy, and serial histology to extend the *Ciona* anatomy and developmental ontology from hatching larva to juvenile stages. They defined 12 new stages, numbered 26–37, and catalogued 203 anatomical entities. 

Adults used for time-lapse and confocal work came from Maizuru Bay and Tokyo Bay in Japan; adults for histology came from the Lagoon of Venice, Italy. These are study-material localities, not a full distribution map.



The phylogenomic study used transcriptomes and genome data from representative species. Its new RNA libraries included mixed samples of two *Salpa fusiformis* blastozooids and 15 *Doliolum nationalis* phorozooids. These counts describe particular sequencing samples, not the number of individuals or species in Tunicata.

## A tunicate larva attaches and absorbs its tail {#card}
In the staged study of Ciona robusta (called C. intestinalis type A in that paper), a swimming larva attaches and absorbs its tail as juvenile anatomy appears. Tail absorption usually took 75–90 minutes at 18–20 °C. These timings belong to the study conditions and one ascidian example, not every tunicate.
