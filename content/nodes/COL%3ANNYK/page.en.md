## Accepted name and placement {#s-71f9fe6f76}
COL26.8 accepts Bufo andrewsi Schmidt, 1925 as a species. Its recorded classification places it along Anura (order) → Bufonidae (family) → Bufo (genus). The name and placement refer to the release issued on 20 August 2026; names in another checklist or fossil database require their own identity evidence.

## Reading route {#s-ab74e91d6c}
Begin with the accepted record and its parent genus, then use the family and order pages to locate related catalogue entries. This page supplies a taxonomic orientation. Species-specific morphology, ecology, life history, distribution, evolutionary evidence and conservation information still require reviewed biological sources.

## A cited muscle-mass comparison is not a new scan result {#s-3f6ff3f296}
The Discussion cites prior andrewsi research reporting greater male muscle mass, interpreted in relation to amplexus and male competition. This paper does not newly dissect, weigh muscles or test mating performance in andrewsi. That earlier result is a proposed functional analogy for gargarizans appendage patterns, not evidence that scan-derived volume equals muscle mass or that every larger limb confers a reproductive advantage.

## A multi-year sample of adults at reproductive sites {#s-71082a2c43}
The study reports 1,824 Bufo andrewsi from seventeen Hengduan Mountain populations in western China, sampled between 2007 and 2013: 1,346 males and 478 females. Toads were hand-captured on land at night while searching for mates or in amplexus, then released after measurement and toe sampling. The sample therefore concerns reproductively active adults under that capture method, not juveniles or a complete population census. Marking of all released animals is not documented, so the stated individual total is not independently certified as distinct animals across years. Source-name agreement does not certify correspondence across later taxonomic revisions.

## The inferred growth season uses an unpublished activity threshold {#s-f786fbe6d8}
Growth-season length is defined as the annual number of days with mean ambient temperature above 6 °C, using meteorological data for 2007–2013. The threshold is based on unpublished field observations, and the authors assume this approximates the activity period. Southern Yunnan populations are described as active from early February to early December, versus early May to mid-October in northern Sichuan, with roughly 150 days between the longest and shortest site estimates. These are regional estimates under that definition, not experimentally established thermal tolerance, a uniform emergence temperature or a verified individual activity diary.

## Laid eggs were measured after local laboratory spawning {#s-b7241f2129}
Amplectant toads from ten populations were taken to nearby laboratories in 2008–2013, kept separately as pairs in pond-water tanks, and allowed to spawn. The Methods give 208 amplectant toads but do not independently establish here whether that number denotes animals, pairs or complete analysed clutches. Researchers counted eggs in each laid clutch and photographed 100 randomly selected eggs per clutch to measure diameter excluding jelly, then returned animals and egg strings to collection sites. This is evidence from tank-spawned clutches, not an ovarian-count extrapolation, field clutch-success survey or hatching experiment.

## Egg size and egg number show an observational trade-off {#s-8797c9a42d}
The Results and Discussion describe larger eggs and smaller clutches in populations with shorter estimated growth seasons, with female size and geographic covariates considered. Clutch size is positively associated with female size and negatively with egg size; one population, YL, departs from the broad pattern. These associations do not directly measure a fixed energy budget, yolk content, offspring fitness or food limitation. The proposed advantages of larger eggs in cold conditions are interpretations, and the original model outputs and local breeding-water temperatures were not independently examined.

## Estimated age has a smaller sample than body-size sampling {#s-809fd0b40e}
The researchers determined LAG-based ages for 1,563 toads, including 1,166 males and 397 females, rather than all 1,824 captured records. They describe resorption checks and differentiation of double and false lines, citing prior validation using four marked–recaptured animals. Known birth dates and the raw bone sections were not independently reconstructed. The youngest and oldest sampled breeding adults are used as proxies for first reproduction and longevity; they are not direct observations of each individual’s first breeding or proof of the species’ maximum lifespan.

## Age contrasts concern sampled breeding populations {#s-0a10b90a6e}
Minimum, mean and maximum sampled ages increase as estimated growth seasons shorten, and females have higher ages than males in these comparisons. The Discussion gives earlier reproduction proxies of 1–2 years and mean reproductive age 5.1 years for some long-season populations, versus 3–4 and 7.1 years in some short-season populations. These values describe the source’s sampled groups, not universal maturation ages for every andrewsi. Predation risk, food availability and temperature-dependent metabolism are proposed explanations rather than measured demographic mechanisms in this study.

## Lifetime activity is a calculated proxy, not tracked life duration {#s-ec0d3c99ea}
The study defines lifetime activity as estimated age in years multiplied by annual growth-season days. The resulting proxy is higher in females and does not significantly vary with season length, latitude or altitude in the source model. It assumes the estimated annual season represents the animal’s life history, without measuring year-by-year activity or actual lifetime exposure. Its relationship with age also follows from the formula, so it cannot independently establish longevity or the energetic cause of body-size variation.

## Body size is associated with age and sex rather than a simple geographic cline {#s-651dc27d9d}
Body length is measured as snout–vent length in millimetres. The study reports sex and age effects on adult size, but no significant prediction from growth-season length, altitude or latitude after adjustment. This is a conditional result in the sampled adults, not proof that temperature never affects body size or that populations are identical. The source does not supply a newly audited complete size range, and the absence of a simple cline does not certify a universal Bergmann rule or its reverse.

## Growth is fitted from age and size, with conflicting directional summaries {#s-af42ee2d19}
Growth parameters are fitted with a von Bertalanffy size-at-age model, rather than longitudinally measured for every toad. Results say male growth rises and female growth tends to fall as growth seasons shorten; Discussion instead emphasizes relatively faster female growth in short seasons and faster males in long seasons, with further allocation statements that are not consistently aligned. These summaries remain unresolved without the fitted data and parameters. They do not establish a directly measured reproductive-energy trade-off or genetically determined growth adaptation.

## The conclusion’s mean-age direction conflicts with the Results {#s-bab1bd7293}
The final Conclusion says longer activity periods have a larger mean age, whereas Results and Discussion describe lower mean age in long-season populations. The Results text also prints a growth-season/clutch-size F value of 4.474 while Table 1 prints 4.046. These discrepancies are not silently corrected or treated as independently reproduced statistics. Geographic variation may involve plasticity and genetic differences; the authors explicitly call for common-garden work, so the field associations do not separate those explanations.

## A mixed field-and-literature inventory in northern Sichuan {#s-7405394da1}
The 2025 paper includes source-labelled Bufo andrewsi in its Tangjiahe National Nature Reserve inventory in northern Sichuan, China. The total list contains 25 species, with 20 from field surveys and five from literature. This page does not assign every table row to a newly captured 2024 animal without the supplementary species-by-species evidence. The field design searched adults on rain-free nights from May to August 2024 using 39 transects, 200–500 m long and 5 m wide, with five trained observers. It is not a year-round census, a juvenile demographic study or an independently checked voucher identification.

## Local habitat assignments are broader than a breeding diagnosis {#s-f5f5cd9284}
Table 1 marks Bufo andrewsi in terrestrial soil-cave; terrestrial farmland; terrestrial highland; running water; quiet water habitats, printing 5 positive categories. Its ticks record source habitat assignments; empty cells are not experimentally demonstrated intolerance or absence from all other habitats. Soil-cave and highland categories are broad labels, not confirmed burrowing physiology or precise elevations. The paper does not provide individual-level abundance or stage-specific breeding observations for every row. Local habitat breadth does not by itself prove evolutionary adaptation, water-quality tolerance or nationwide habitat exclusivity.

## Regional gradients and survey gains do not certify recovery {#s-06f1a7b74c}
The reserve description spans 1,150–3,830 m, the sampling Methods report 800–2,600 m, and statistical Methods describe observed amphibians at 800–2,500 m. These scopes are retained rather than assigned as this species’ measured range. Richness peaks in two regional bands but its linear elevation association is nonsignificant (reported R² 0.114, p 0.412). Historical recorded-species increase is also nonsignificant (Mann–Kendall p 0.148). Neither result establishes unchanged populations, increased abundance or a causal benefit of management. Discussion and Conclusions use stronger fragmentation, conservation-success and large-population language than these tests alone support; raw records and historical sampling comparability have not been independently reanalysed.
