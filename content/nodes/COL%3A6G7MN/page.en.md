## Collection and chemical interpretation limits {#s-9f62927bb1}
The GC-MS sample of Epipedobates anthonyi belongs to the 2014 Ecuador/Colombia collection series. Whole skins were placed in methanol after lidocaine euthanasia; species sample sizes were opportunistic, with random subsets chosen within Epipedobates. Standards were unavailable for most frog alkaloids, so absolute quantities cannot be recovered from these reported signal values. The two instrument/pipeline datasets are not directly comparable. Full-skin GC-MS results were not mass-corrected, and co-eluting compounds with the same base peak could not always be distinguished. The authors did not serially sample each species across seasons and a broad range. Raw chromatograms, vouchers and supplementary individual-level records have not been independently reanalysed here.

## Passive accumulation remains a mechanistic hypothesis {#s-d1c54ff920}
The paper uses these skin observations in a proposed sequence from dietary toxin exposure and some resistance, through slower elimination and temporary retention, to derived transport or storage associated with sequestration. For Epipedobates anthonyi, a measured skin profile alone does not establish dietary origin, elimination rate, a binding mechanism or transport specifically to skin. Presence in skin does not by itself distinguish passive retention from specialised sequestration. The proposed ancestral condition and evolutionary sequence require broader sampling and tissue/time-series experiments; they are not direct observations of ancestors or a reconstructed molecular pathway in this sampled species.

## Species-specific detections and their evidence limits {#s-d5f6e131b1}
The paper reports trace epibatidine in the one examined individual of this species. Epibatidine can co-elute with methyl palmitoleate in GC-MS and produce false positives; the authors therefore checked LC-HRMS against a standard. This detection is distinct from the article’s cited epibatidine-feeding experiments and does not quantify a natural toxic dose or resistance in the sampled frog.

## Gland types describe structure rather than chemical identity {#s-1680cbada4}
Type I glands are chiefly alveolar, with a small lumen, a single layer of secretory cells and an incomplete myoepithelial covering; they lie near the epidermis. Type II glands have a more tubular-alveolar or tear-shaped secretory portion and are restricted to the swollen part of finger IV in the examined dendrobatids. They may be interspersed among type I glands or lie beneath them; that arrangement varies among individuals. The definitions combine morphology and histochemical reactions; they do not identify a particular secreted molecule or demonstrate a physiological effect.

## Preserved material, finger numbering and staining {#s-76680aed77}
No new specimens were collected for this study. Its dendrobatoid material comprised 77 specimens representing 36 source species labels; four additional specimens represented three hylodid species, and two Melanophryniscus outgroups were taken from literature. Exact preservation histories were unknown, although the authors understood the specimens to have been formalin-fixed and stored in ethanol. They usually removed one hand, decalcified it in EDTA and made 4.5 micrometre resin sections. Multiple stains tested tissue structure, neutral and acidic mucopolysaccharides, proteins and lipids. Fingers are numbered II–V under a tetrapod homology convention, so finger IV is the third of the four remaining digits. These methods describe the comparative study; they do not supply a species-specific sample size in the inspected main-text table. Appendix S1 and Tables S1–S2 remain unread, and figures were inspected only as captions.

## A proposed reproductive role remains untested {#s-cad8d5ef39}
The authors suggest that protein-containing specialized hand glands could mediate chemical courtship signals during contact with a female's head. They did not characterize the secretions, identify a pheromone, test female responses or establish whether delivery is olfactory or transdermal. Hand location and stain reactions therefore cannot certify that Epipedobates anthonyi uses a particular chemical mating mechanism, causes ovulation or oviposition, or secretes an adhesive. A lack of these gland types likewise does not prove a species lacks every form of chemical communication. Behavioral observations cited in the discussion come from earlier studies and populations, not new mating experiments in this histological comparison.
