## Eight juveniles sampled over eight force-plate sessions {#s-fcd3f86063}
At Zoo Atlanta, Chang and Ting (2017) recorded ground-reaction forces and video from eight juvenile Phoenicopterus chilensis across eight sessions. The juveniles weighed 0.25–1.40 kg and had leg lengths of 0.17–0.40 m; these are study-sample ranges, not adult species ranges. The force plate sampled at 200 Hz, and video-derived toe positions helped estimate the centre of pressure, the point under the foot where the measured force acts. Eight sessions and eight birds are separate counts, not sixteen individuals.

## Smaller pressure-centre excursions when quiet with eyes closed {#s-6c34b2a4e0}
When the juveniles stood quietly on one leg with their eyes closed, the centre of pressure stayed near the tarsometatarsophalangeal joint and moved less than during activity. The main text reports 95% excursion radii of 3.2 ± 0.5 mm when quiescent and 5.1 ± 0.4 mm when active; each radius contains 95% of the measured force-location movements. These values describe the resultant force under the foot, not movement of the body’s centre of mass or a fixed threshold for the species. The representative bird shown in a figure has separate individual values.

## A state transition in one twenty-minute recording {#s-6b497e8e3c}
One highlighted continuous 20-minute recording shows a bird first standing with its eyes open, then becoming quiet with eyes closed as its centre of pressure became more localized. The authors suggest it may have fallen asleep, based on behaviour; they did not stage sleep using brain recordings. This single example does not establish continuous sleep duration in the wild or resting posture across ages, surfaces and weather.

## Balance trajectories cannot replace muscle recordings {#s-10bbc255da}
Localized loading and small excursions are consistent with balance requiring little muscle activity, but the authors could not exclude sustained, high muscle coactivation. The study reports no direct muscle-activity or metabolic measurements. Keep the juvenile live-bird force-plate results separate from joint angles measured in adult P. ruber cadavers. Passive support is an experimentally supported candidate mechanism; muscular effort in live birds remains unmeasured.
