## A plant-eating lizard with two life-history records {#s-34cfa8d677}
The ReptTraits record describes Uromastyx dispar as a ground-dwelling, plant-eating agamid active by day. It reports a maximum snout–vent length of 232 mm and classifies the species as egg-laying. A separate 2015 life-history archive includes measurements and summaries for a same-name entry; the two compilations use different fields and methods.

## Maximum length excludes the tail {#s-3eef6e9bcd}
The ReptTraits row gives a reported maximum snout–vent length of 232 mm and a female maximum of 212 mm. This measure runs from the snout to the vent and leaves out the tail. These compiled maxima are not average adult sizes or a growth limit; the sex-specific values may come from different places or studies, so their difference alone cannot quantify size variation within a population.

## On the ground, eating plants by day {#s-4acb02c729}
The cited row describes activity on the ground, a herbivorous diet, and daytime activity. “Herbivorous” indicates plant food without naming particular plants. The labels do not map every habitat or establish a fixed daily schedule; habits can vary among populations, seasons, and life stages.

## Egg counts are summary values {#s-d211b8ed22}
The workbook classifies Uromastyx dispar as egg-laying. Its consolidated clutch mean is 17, the lowest and highest reported means are 10 and 24, and the record gives one clutch per year. These summaries can be midpoints between published means—or between reported extremes when means are unavailable—rather than averages of observed nests. They are not guaranteed counts for each clutch or proof that all populations reproduce alike.

## A historical life-history entry under the same name {#s-1a3c314bdc}
The August 2015 archive has a same-name, same-family species entry with six source-linked life-history fields. Because that archive can inherit data across historical species and subspecies, a matching name does not prove that every value refers to the exact COL26.8 concept of Uromastyx dispar.

## Adult and hatching mass describe different stages {#s-b48d5b65de}
The archive summarizes adult body mass as 500 g and mass at birth or hatching as 4 g. The adult value can combine males, females, and adults of unspecified sex. The two figures refer to different life stages; they do not track the same individuals as they grow or give the full range of body masses in the species.

## Maturity, clutch size, and incubation {#s-2b1f97ce20}
The historical table gives 1,461 days for the female maturity field, which its source defines as age at first conception. It also records 13 offspring per litter or eggs per clutch and 95 days from egg laying to hatching. The clutch figure is a separate archive summary from the ReptTraits value above; the fields combine litters and clutches, so their numbers should not be treated as interchangeable. The 95 days measure incubation to hatching, not a full reproductive cycle.

## A 24-year longevity record is not a typical lifespan {#s-4cb278cd40}
The archive’s maximum-longevity field is 24 years, the largest record found under its compilation method. It is not a typical lifespan, a mean lifespan in the wild, a limit for every individual, or a current record known to be repeatedly attained.

## Separate source labels support separate fields {#s-2856b91769}
The historical table’s source labels include Meiri (2010), Meiri, Brown, and Sibly (2011), BC Reptiles (Meyers et al., 2009), and the *Reptile Incubation* book. Different fields draw on different sources, and a label may refer to a paper, book, or database. Row counts are not numbers of measured animals, and repeated citations do not represent independent experiments.

## How to interpret the compiled values {#s-1125d35ad9}
ReptTraits and the 2015 life-history archive are separate compilations. Both condense differences among sex, age, locality, and population; the older archive can also inherit values across historical species or subspecies. Its summaries use medians or averages of the two middle values, except for maximum longevity, which uses the largest collected record. They are not direct measurements of one animal, individual variation ranges, mean wild lifespans, or current population surveys. The archive does not consistently establish whether records came from wild or captive settings, and its labels do not prove that historical names match every modern taxonomic concept. The source material also does not update conservation status or current distribution.
