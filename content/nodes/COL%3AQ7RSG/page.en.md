## Accepted name and placement {#s-1b053525cf}
COL26.8 accepts Limnonectes phyllofolia Frederick, Iskandar, Riyanto, Hamidy, Reilly, Stubbs, Bloch, Bach & McGuire, 2023 as a species. Its recorded classification places it along Anura (order) → Dicroglossidae (family) → Limnonectes (genus). The name and placement refer to the release issued on 20 August 2026; names in another checklist or fossil database require their own identity evidence.

## Small size, reduced webbing and bony mandibular processes {#s-bfa9bac297}
The paper described phyllofolia as a small Sulawesi fanged frog with greatly reduced toe webbing and a postorbital groove, while finger webbing was absent in the holotype. The holotype male had SVL 28.12 mm and a 1.14-mm odontoid process, a fang-like bony projection of the mandible rather than a measured dental tooth. The comparisons round maximum SVL to 30 mm but later give 30.13 mm; the rounded claim is not an exact biological ceiling. Dorsal skin roughness and live colour varied across specimens. The reported body-size range overlaps arathooni, so small size alone is not a sufficient field identification.

## Three confirmed localities and uncertain broader occupancy {#s-d637e49b63}
The description knew phyllofolia from Bontomaranu on Lompobatang, Balease and Bontosiri in Bantimurung National Park, spanning the Southwest Peninsula and southeastern Central Core across the Tempe Depression. It reported locality elevations 495–1,173 m, whereas the natural-history discussion emphasized a likely below-1,100-m association and called the Lompobatang site an outlier. These statements are retained as measured records versus an interpretive generalization. Predicted occupation up to 1,200–1,300 m and hypothesized historic lowland distribution are not confirmed sites. Failure to detect frogs in higher forest does not establish an exact upper physiological limit.

## Nine male-associated nests and an explicit clutch-range difference {#s-75ebdf6f0c}
Nine nests collected with adult males averaged fifteen eggs, observed range 10–21. The comparison paragraph instead summarizes nests as 10–20 eggs, so the narrower summary is not substituted for the explicit nest sample. Eggs were approximately five millimetres in diameter, tightly packed in jelly, on leaves or wet moss-covered boulders over or near water. This is terrestrial egg deposition linked to aquatic habitats, not demonstrated direct development or live birth. Cloudy late-stage or nonviable eggs and suggested fungal susceptibility without a guardian do not establish that every unattended nest dies or quantify the benefit of attendance.

## Nighttime nest attendance with unconfirmed dual-clutch guarding {#s-afa0589863}
Males were observed on egg clutches at night at Balease and Bontosiri; unattended clutches were seen by day at Balease. Close spacing led the authors to suggest one male might attend two clutches, but they never observed a male switching between them. Attending males did not call, while three observed calling males were not on or near clutches. These are encounter patterns, not evidence of permanent attendance, genetic paternity, a fixed division of parental roles or measured defence efficiency. Amplexus was not witnessed, leaving which sex selected the laying site unresolved.

## Leaf-litter encounters far from a known breeding seep {#s-009d132b9c}
Several Balease individuals were collected hundreds of metres from the identified breeding seep in open forest leaf litter. That seep flowed for roughly fifty metres before disappearing, with no known continuous stream within 1.5 km. Other breeding records involved leaves or mossy rocks over slow water, but some attended nests were beside a wider faster stream or a nearly still pool. The authors inferred a largely terrestrial ecomorph and concern over mature lowland forest scarcity; these observations do not quantify competition, prey use, survival in villages or strict avoidance of every larger stream. Different-quality known sites remain exceptions to a simple pristine-only claim.

## Sixteen calls from three males rather than sixteen independent animals {#s-ab82bf78dd}
Sixteen phyllofolia calls from three males were analysed. Calls were rapid clicks with 11–54 notes, duration 0.596–2.86 seconds and pulse rate 16.78–19.44 notes per second. Dominant frequency ranged 1,142.70–2,818.89 Hz. Duration and frequency overlap the arathooni comparison, while the sampled note counts and pulse rates were more distinct; no single frequency threshold is supplied as a universal key. Repeated calls from a male are not separate animals, and multivariate differences in this small recording sample do not demonstrate female preference, mate rejection or all-population acoustic invariance.
