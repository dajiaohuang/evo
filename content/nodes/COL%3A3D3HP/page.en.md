## A cited one-of-two host record cannot define species prevalence {#s-329d3cbb5b}
The 2023 parasite account lists spelaea as an additional Sphyranura euryceae host and cites an earlier report of one infected individual among two examined, expressed as 50% prevalence. This is a very small historical host sample, not the present study’s tynerensis survey or a representative estimate for spelaea throughout its range. The paper does not newly sample spelaea populations, establish infection intensity for this species or measure health consequences. Association with the parasite does not convert the worm’s Oklahoma type locality or Arkansas sampling spring into a new confirmed spelaea distribution record.

## The worm’s molecular traits are not salamander traits {#s-b8c5ca2a45}
The paper revises the diagnosis and molecular placement of the monogenean flatworm Sphyranura euryceae. Listed ribosomal and mitochondrial accessions describe parasites, not the salamander host’s genome. The account places worms on external gills and skin, especially near the leg bases; a host association does not establish exclusive host specificity, physiological tolerance or a measured effect on survival. Parasite prevalence is the proportion of examined hosts carrying worms, whereas intensity is the number of worms on an infected host. Neither supplies the host’s abundance, fertility, life span or current conservation category.

## Adult tapeworm associations in a historical North American checklist {#s-b44a9c0706}
The 2023 review includes Eurycea spelaea among amphibian hosts with records of adult intestinal cestodes. Its named parasite associations are Bothriocephalus rarus, Bothriocephalus typhlotritonis, Ophiotaenia amphiumae. These describe compiled host–parasite records, not parasites measured in every member of the species. The scope is adult worms in wild continental North American herpetofauna; larval cestode records are excluded. An intestinal association alone does not establish disease severity, host mortality, zoonotic transmission or the species’ role in completing every parasite life cycle. Prevalence, intensity, examined-host denominators and experimental susceptibility are not supplied by this host checklist.

## How to interpret the compiled parasite evidence {#s-f8aabd052b}
The authors assembled literature records into both parasite-first and host-first lists and supplied an identification key using earlier taxonomic works. The host-first list intentionally omits worms identified only to genus; a shorter list there is not evidence that genus-level records do not exist. The article reports 88 named amphibian hosts overall, alongside unresolved and hybrid labels in its host list; that source total is not the number of pinned COL26.8 pages linked here. This page uses only an exact source-name match to the frozen accepted name, without certifying every historical specimen’s identity. The source does not document a dated, reproducible database-query protocol sufficient to treat a missing association as a systematic negative finding.
