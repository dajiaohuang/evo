## Grey preserved regions within a complete reconstructed outline {#s-a02daaeeaf}
Figures 1 and 2 in the 2013 study separate preserved skull fragments from the reconstructed outline: grey marks the bones actually present. Treat the rostral tips, openings and teeth outside those grey areas as reconstructed, not fully preserved. Figure 3 shows surface grooves interpreted in its caption as vascular impressions. The grooves are visible; their vascular origin and proposed cooling role are interpretations. No measurements were taken from the scale bars.

## Why the large specimen retains a cf. identification {#s-cc5e1fbae2}
The authors identify MN 6594-V as Tropeognathus cf. T. mesembrinus. The cranial resemblance supports comparison with the species, while cf. signals that the identification is not certain. Crest differences could reflect growth or individual variation, but the study does not exclude other explanations. The holotype is mainly skull material, so it cannot supply limb proportions for this specimen.

## Concretion preservation does not recover the exact collecting site {#s-9633023478}
MN 6594-V preserves parts of the skull and jaw, spine, shoulder girdle, sternum, pelvis and wings in concretions; only one femur represents the hindlimbs. It was collected earlier by local residents, and its exact site is unknown. Nodule features point to the Romualdo Formation, with the Santana do Cariri area proposed as a likely source. Calcite veins, weathering and compression displaced or deformed bones, and the tip of the snout is missing. A probable source is not a precise locality or a complete, undistorted skeleton.

## Crests and alveoli preserve form while function remains interpretive {#s-6f5ae4a3e6}
The comparison considers a differently inclined premaxillary crest, a low blunt frontoparietal crest, occipital orientation and a palatal ridge. The study interprets surface grooves as vascular impressions and discusses cooling as a possible function; neither the vessels nor thermoregulatory behaviour are directly preserved. Damage also leaves openings and tooth rows incomplete, so visible tooth sockets do not give a complete tooth count or diet.

## The preserved body records support and missing segments {#s-0060863e75}
The authors describe twelve dorsal vertebrae. Five centra are fused into a notarium, although some neural spines remain separate; five sacral vertebrae join the ilium, and the scapula–coracoid and carpal series are fused. Humerus and forearm shafts are incomplete, only the near end of the wing metacarpal remains, and the pelvis is partial. These features support comparisons of joints and trunk support, not a fully measured wing or a universal growth sequence.

## Sampled histology supports growth approaching cessation {#s-183d4d9eb0}
Histology comes from the distal end of the first wing phalanx, and calcite obscures some of the section. The authors describe an external fundamental system, parallel circumferential tissue and growth marks, and infer that growth had stopped or was nearly complete. Some axial bones remain unfused. One local sample cannot provide a continuous growth record, an exact age or the species’ maximum adult size.

## The 8.70 m wingspan includes comparative reconstruction {#s-f14be2fdc2}
The study estimates missing bone lengths from preserved bone ends and proportions in other pterosaurs, then combines wing, wrist and shoulder-girdle measurements. It reports a mean “maximized wingspan” of about 8.70 m and a “normal wingspan” of about 8.26 m, the latter applying a proposed 5% reduction for joint flexion. These are modelled values for the specimen, not spans measured between intact wingtips or a species maximum. Comparator choices affect the result; 5% is not a universal pterosaur rule.

## Other large wing bones retain family-level uncertainty {#s-15760a3e57}
The same study discusses MCT 1838-R, an isolated distal humerus, and MPSC R 1395, a partial left wing. Both remain indeterminate Anhangueridae rather than becoming Tropeognathus mesembrinus because of their size. An unfused extensor process in the MPSC wing informs the authors’ discussion of immaturity. These pieces cannot be combined into an ideal adult or used to extend this species’ size range.

## The Gondwana size claim retains its 2013 comparison scope {#s-d40fdcffbf}
The 2013 title’s claim of the largest flying reptile from Gondwana belongs to that paper’s material and comparisons. Its “giant” threshold of more than 7.4 m is an explicitly arbitrary working cut-off, not a biological group or a current independently checked record. A few large finds do not reveal population abundance or a coastal habitat preference.

This page combines selected observations and models from the 2013 study with one PBDB collection.
