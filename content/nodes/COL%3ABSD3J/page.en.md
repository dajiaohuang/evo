## Habitat flexibility in older-name records {#s-c87004fc79}
Heymann and Aquino reviewed records from the Peruvian Amazon and found that most known sites were in non-flooded terra firme forest or mixed habitats, so the Ucayali bald uacari should not be summarized as a flooded-forest specialist. They also note that their material combines published work, unpublished reports and personal observations. The highest encounter rate came from the relatively high-elevation, less disturbed Sierras de Contamana, so habitat quality cannot be separated from lower human disturbance as an explanation. The article uses the older name C. calvus ucayalii.

## Calls and tail wagging in one wild population {#s-c3972673d5}
León and Bowler classified 1,155 vocalizations from one wild population using acoustic and behavioral analyses. They described 12 distinguishable call types, including two not previously reported, High-chick and Shriek; Hic was the most common call. They also observed calls paired with tail wagging, suggesting that visual and acoustic signals can work together. The findings provide a baseline for acoustic monitoring at a local population, not a complete communication inventory for the species.

## Evidence limits {#s-c9575479c2}
This page combines an older-name habitat review, one-population vocal research and long-term seasonal encounters. Sites, dates and sampling methods differ; none provides a modern range-wide census. Ripe-fruit scores do not directly measure immature-seed availability, and seasonal movement needs tracking. Population trends, annual diet and species-wide behavior remain incompletely documented..

## Seasonal sightings across nearly four decades {#s-71784d87b9}
In 2025, Heymann, Flores Amasifuén and Shahuano Tello compiled records from Quebrada Blanco in Peru. Their methods report 56 sightings in 1985–2022, mostly supplied by resident field assistants. The approximately 1 km² study area is primarily terra firme forest, with small swampy patches along creeks. These are encounters as animals passed through the site, not 56 individuals. Opportunistic reports across decades do not establish density or a population trend.

## More sightings in the dry season, with limits to correlation {#s-0e9f5edb16}
In two-month bins, sightings peaked in September–October and were least frequent in January–April. Counts were significantly negatively correlated with rainfall (Spearman r = −0.89, p < 0.05); the negative correlation with ripe-fruit scores was not significant (r = −0.77). These six seasonal bins do not show that rainfall caused animals to leave, nor do they adjust all years and months for observation effort. Rainfall seasonality came from Tamshiyacu, about 40 km away, rather than weather measured at each sighting.

## Ripe-fruit scores are an indirect food indicator {#s-25a54036c3}
Fruit scores came from 216 trees monitored in 1997–1998, 2000–2001 and 2005. Plants were selected because tamarins and titi monkeys used them. The scores did not directly measure the availability of immature seeds eaten by uakaris. The authors shifted the phenology series by four months using evidence from other Amazonian regions; this lag was not measured locally at Quebrada Blanco.

## Movement between habitats remains a hypothesis {#s-251bc7ddf2}
The authors suggest that seasonal encounters may reflect movement between terra firme and Amazon floodplain forest. March–May 2020 sightings at nearby ARC came from personal communication, separate from the 56-record series. Distances of about 6 km to floodplain forest and 12 km to ARC are straight-line separations, not tracked routes. The authors explicitly call the scenario hypothetical and propose prolonged tracking; drones are a suggestion. Protection may need to accommodate seasonal habitat use, but the study did not measure a minimum reserve size.
