## Cephalochordata: a living branch, not an ancestor portrait {#overview}
COL26.8 records Cephalochordata (7NF2Q) as a subphylum under Chordata. The studies here examine living lancelets rather than an ancestral specimen: one tracks cartilage-related gene expression in embryos and larvae; another compares a Florida lancelet genome with vertebrate chromosomes. Together they show how modern cephalochordates can help test chordate history, provided observations in a sampled animal stay distinct from reconstructions of deep ancestors.

## Gene activity in the pharyngeal arch {#pharyngeal-arch}
Meulemans and Bronner-Fraser examined 11 amphioxus gene orthologs in embryos and larvae from a 12-hour early neurula to a 2.5-day feeding larva with two to three gill slits. Their Figure 4 shows the collagen gene ColA expressed in the mesoderm of the first pharyngeal arch in a 1.5-day larva. The study reports that larval pharyngeal mesoderm co-expressed Twist, Ets, Alx and ColA, but not SoxE or SoxD, two factors required for vertebrate cartilage formation. The authors therefore did not find a complete vertebrate-like cartilage program in that tissue. These are gene-expression observations; they do not by themselves prove gene function or structural homology. The Methods passage read does not name the amphioxus species, so the result cannot be assigned here to every cephalochordate.

## The branch beside tunicates and vertebrates {#chordate-relationships}
Putnam and colleagues compared intron presence and absence at 5,337 aligned coding positions and reported Bayesian support for tunicates and vertebrates as sister groups. In that analysis, cephalochordates lie outside their shared branch. This is a phylogenetic inference from the study’s sampled genes and taxa, not a claim that a living lancelet is a vertebrate ancestor.

## Seventeen reconstructed linkage groups {#chromosome-history}
A comparison of the Branchiostoma floridae genome with vertebrate genomes identified 17 ancestral chordate linkage groups: blocks of genes that remain linked, not preserved ancestral chromosomes. From their pattern, the authors inferred two whole-genome duplications on the vertebrate stem, after cephalochordates had diverged and before the teleost–tetrapod split. The paper could not resolve whether the duplications were widely separated or close to the lamprey lineage split. The timing and ancestral blocks are comparative reconstructions, not directly observed events.

## One animal behind a genome comparison {#genome-sample}
The 2008 genome sequence began with DNA from the gonads of one gravid male Branchiostoma floridae collected in Tampa Bay, Florida, in July 2003. That specimen anchors the study’s genome assembly; it does not measure variation across the species or across Cephalochordata. A useful next question is whether broader lancelet sampling preserves the same reconstructed linkage groups or reveals changes among lineages.

## Gene activity in a lancelet pharyngeal arch {#card}
In amphioxus larvae, a study found the collagen gene ColA expressed in the first pharyngeal arch’s mesoderm at the 1.5-day larval stage. The sampled tissue did not express two factors for vertebrate cartilage formation, so the authors did not find a complete vertebrate-like cartilage program. Gene expression alone does not prove function or homology.
