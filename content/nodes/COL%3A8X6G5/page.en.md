## Teeth, scales and an early chondrichthyan history {#reading-introduction}
What can isolated teeth tell us about an animal whose complete body is missing? Qianodus tooth whorls, Fanjingshania dermal elements and the single Gladbachus specimen preserve different parts of early chondrichthyan anatomy. Comparing them requires keeping each sample and its preservation separate.

## The Chondrichthyes skeleton is not all cartilage {#s-62b54f081e}
The name cartilaginous fishes can hide the hard parts in their fossil record. Chondrichthyan material includes mineralized teeth and scale denticles, as well as fin spines, dermal plates and calcified cartilage. Each fossil records only the tissues and body regions actually preserved.

## Qianodus tooth whorls preserve a feeding structure {#s-2e58a76291}
At about 439 Ma, Qianodus duplicis is represented by 23 isolated tooth whorls, catalogued as IVPP V26641–V26663. The whorls preserve paired rows of non-shedding teeth added from the tongue side in an offset pattern. They record early jawed-vertebrate dentition, but no associated jaw or body is known from these specimens. The Atlas uses the sample as an early stem-line evidence anchor, not as a crown-group first appearance or a direct ancestor.

## Fanjingshania preserves spines, plates and scales {#s-85fd974913}
The *Fanjingshania renovata* material comes from the Shiqian–Tunping section at Leijiatun, Guizhou, in the Rongxi Formation's *Ozarkodina guizhouensis* conodont biozone. The authors assign the horizon to the upper Aeronian, about 439 Ma, within the *O. parahassi* division of that biozone; they discuss lower Telychian estimates as an alternative linked to diachrony in the formation. The sample consists of separate dermal plates, scales and fin spines, including pectoral, pelvic, dorsal, anal and prepelvic spines, not one articulated skeleton.

Histology distinguishes these structures: fin-spine sections show calcified cartilage and cellular bone, while scale odontodes show resorption and replacement. The study reports no odontogenic tissue in the spines. A fin spine, a scale crown and a tooth whorl therefore document different structures, even when all are mineralized.

## Gladbachus complicates the familiar shark body plan {#s-57f6c8ca3b}
The single known Gladbachus adentatus specimen, UMZC 2000.32, comes from the Givetian Lower Plattenkalk of Germany, about 385 Ma. CT imaging and histology reveal parts of the skull, jaws, gill skeleton, pectoral girdle, cartilage, scales and teeth. Its combination of characters differs from a simple modern-shark template, and the study places it on the chondrichthyan stem.

The authors infer that it may have suspension-fed from its skeletal anatomy; that is a functional hypothesis, not an observed meal. Together, these separated Silurian and Devonian samples raise a broader question: which parts of the later shark-like body plan were inherited, and which appeared independently during early chondrichthyan evolution? They do not establish a continuous fossil range or a direct line of ancestry.
