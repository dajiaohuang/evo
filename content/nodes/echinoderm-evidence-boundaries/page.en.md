## From echinoderm fragments to crown time {#s-4ca5ef6bdb}
Follow five separate records, from Cambrian skeletal mesh to articulated fossils, class-level appearances and clock-based crown ages.

## What an isolated stereom fragment can show {#s-ebb383df41}
Cambrian Stage 3 ossicles preserve stereom, the porous calcitic mesh that makes up an echinoderm skeleton. But an isolated ossicle cannot reveal a complete body plan, and it does not date the phylum’s origin.

## Why Yanjiahella remains a candidate {#s-37c7f5628b}
One analysis placed Yanjiahella on the echinoderm stem, but a published reanalysis found neither stereom nor an unambiguous shared derived trait, or synapomorphy, linking it to echinoderms. The proposed Fortunian occurrence therefore remains contested; it cannot set a secure first appearance.

## Articulated bodies provide a firmer comparison {#s-597bab1b7c}
By about 510 Ma, articulated echinoderm fossils—bodies whose parts remain connected—show at least four markedly different body plans. These are stronger body-level evidence than loose stereom fragments, but they show diversification already under way, not the phylum’s exact first appearance.

## Each echinoderm class has its own fossil record {#s-16722e4c85}
The unproblematic crinoid record begins in the middle Tremadocian, whereas the oldest articulated holothurian in this dossier is late Darriwilian. Those class-level bounds come from different specimens and strata; neither one stands in for the whole phylum.

## Separate fossil relatives from crown-age models {#s-3dbf53acf7}
Darriwilian bothriocidaroid plates anchor total-group Echinoidea: the living crown and its extinct stem relatives. Phylogenomic clock analyses place the crown near the Carboniferous–Permian transition, but that divergence time is model-dependent. A fossil occurrence and an inferred split answer different questions.
