## atlas-profile / overview {#s-3d15332921}
The accepted name Homo sapiens covers a species history far wider than any one fossil or genome. Here, bones, cave chemistry and ancient DNA open several different windows; each has its own place, dating method and limits.

## atlas-profile / evidenceSummary {#s-f6a6685769}
The examples range from two Tam Pà Ling fossils and a Ranis diet study to one Siberian genome and a southern African genomic sample. Their ages and methods answer different questions; together they do not reveal a complete species range, origin date or migration history.

## Different materials answer different questions {#s-f531560279}
A frontal-bone fragment, a proximal tibia, ancient genomes and collagen isotopes are different kinds of evidence about ancient Homo sapiens. The Laos fossils have ages estimated from surrounding sediments; a Siberian femur was directly dated; and cave collagen from Germany was analyzed for dietary signals. Keeping each result tied to its material, method and place prevents separate observations from turning into one continuous story.

## No single study covers the species' history {#s-6b30405a0f}
The name Homo sapiens is the accepted species name in the pinned COL26.8 checklist. The studies here examine particular fossils, archaeological settings and population-genomic samples. They address different questions and cannot be combined into a complete account of human origins, present-day diversity or migration.

## How old are the Tam Pà Ling fossils? {#s-37c18714c4}
At Tam Pà Ling cave in northern Laos, the study attributes TPL 6, a left frontal-bone fragment, and TPL 7, the upper end of a left tibia, to H. sapiens. Models based on surrounding sediments estimate 70 ± 3 thousand years for TPL 6 and as old as 77 ± 9 thousand years for TPL 7. These are contextual estimates rather than direct dates on the bones; the authors note that TPL 7 may be older still, and alteration after burial limits its measurement.

## What the Ranis cave samples say about diet {#s-322a3eb93f}
At Ilsenhöhle in Ranis, Germany, researchers measured stable isotopes in collagen—a protein preserved in bone—from 10 human remains and compared them with 52 animal remains from the same archaeological setting. The results point to a diet dominated by large land mammals in a cold steppe–tundra environment. The layers date to about 47,500–45,770 and 46,820–43,260 calibrated years before present; those ages are not direct dates for each human bone. One cave cannot define human diets generally.

## A genomic comparison south of the Limpopo {#s-20b7d2059e}
One whole-genome study analyzed 28 people from archaeological sites south of the Limpopo River in central and southern Africa, spanning 10,200 to 150 calibrated years before present. In that study's comparison panel, samples older than 1,400 years fell outside the genetic-variation range represented by its modern-human samples and carried many variants the authors classified as specific to H. sapiens. The result depends on which individuals and modern comparison samples were included and how the authors defined those variants.

## One directly dated person from Ust’-Ishim {#s-c8e859803a}
The Ust’-Ishim 1 femur from western Siberia has a direct radiocarbon age of 46,880–43,210 calibrated years before present at 95.4% probability. The genome comes from this one person. This date does not give the origin of H. sapiens or the species' first and last appearances.

## These sites do not form one migration route {#s-eafe524fba}
The Laos fossils, Ranis diet signal, Siberian individual and southern African genomes differ in place, dating method and research question. They offer several views of ancient H. sapiens, but they do not trace one continuous migration route or define the species' current geographic range.

## atlas-profile / readerLimitations {#s-851d15b5f8}
These examples cover only the fossils and datasets named above. They do not describe all living populations, the species' full life history, present-day range or origin. Sediment-based estimates, direct fossil dates and genomic comparisons have different meanings and should remain distinct.
