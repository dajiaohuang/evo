## Sticklebacks as a comparison system {#brief}
Sticklebacks are a useful comparison group partly because their species occupy different combinations of marine, brackish and fresh water. A nine-species study combined mitochondrial genomes with eleven nuclear genes and found that several familiar similarities could reflect repeated adaptation to marine habitats rather than close ancestry.

## Different species occupy different waters {#habitats}
The habitat summary accompanying Kawahara and colleagues’ sampled tree records Gasterosteus aculeatus and Pungitius pungitius in marine, brackish and freshwater settings; G. wheatlandi and Apeltes quadracus in marine and brackish settings; Culaea inconstans as freshwater-restricted; and Spinachia spinachia as marine-restricted. These are the paper’s comparative records for named species, not a claim that each species uses all habitats throughout its range.

## More sequence data changed the stickleback tree {#family-relationships}
Kawahara and colleagues analysed a 14,807-base-pair mitochondrial genome dataset and 8,703 base pairs across 11 single-copy nuclear genes. The two sequence datasets supported the same revised topology, including a different placement for Spinachia than earlier hypotheses that treated it as the earliest living stickleback lineage. Their interpretation was that similarities between Spinachia and aulorhynchid fishes (Aulorhynchus flavidus and Aulichthys japonicus) likely arose independently in similar marine habitats. The conclusion depends on the sampled species and gene evidence in this study.
