## What male forms does the study describe? {#s-b7f1bf54a9}
The 2024 article's genus-level introduction describes male orangutans as flanged or unflanged. It says flanged males are larger and have secondary sexual traits that unflanged males lack. These are background descriptions of Pongo, not measurements of the study's Pongo pygmaeus wurmbii population or a range-wide diagnosis of Pongo pygmaeus.

## How many offspring did the flanged males sire? {#s-e1267dcbc0}
At Gunung Palung National Park in West Kalimantan, four flanged males sired five offspring during the study's stated 2009–2014 period. In the broader sample of offspring conceived from 2008 to 2019, paternity was assigned for five of seven offspring. An additional assigned case was conceived before that period, and the sire's flange status at conception was unknown. These are results from one completely wild Pongo pygmaeus wurmbii population, not a species-wide reproductive rate.

## How were orangutan numbers estimated from nests? {#s-e48de548e1}
At ten survey locations inside and around Danau Sentarum National Park, transect surveys from 2010 to 2014 were combined with nest-decay observations from 2010–2011. Using an updated mean nest-decay time of 288.3 days, the study modelled about 202 orangutans inside the park (95% CI 75.67–541.79) and 71 outside (95% CI 20.76–244.63). These are estimates for the surveyed landscape, not direct counts; they depend on the nest-decay assumption and survey locations.

## What did the reintroduced lineages show? {#s-cbb5a40421}
At Camp Leakey in Central Kalimantan, researchers analyzed mitochondrial DNA from ten presumed unrelated adults reintroduced in the 1970s and 1980s, plus one offspring sequence representing a deceased reintroduced female. Of eleven profiles representing founder lineages, nine clustered with the local Pongo pygmaeus wurmbii mitochondrial lineage; two females clustered with Pongo pygmaeus pygmaeus from northern West Kalimantan or Sarawak, and both reproduced with local males. The paper reported at least 22 hybridized or introgressed descendants, at least 15 considered alive when it was published in 2016. This is a human-mediated history at one release site, not evidence of natural gene flow across the species' range.

## Was the Usun Apau sighting evidence of a range expansion? {#s-8a7f1da254}
During twelve wildlife surveys from October 2017 to October 2020, researchers reported one roughly one-minute orangutan sighting near Libut Camp in Usun Apau National Park, Sarawak, on 17 September 2020 at about 1,020 m elevation. They also reported four nearby nests—three new and one old—and two vocalizations. The authors treated this as a locality outside the Sarawak range shown on maps available to them at the time. One sighting does not establish a resident population or confirm range expansion; the individual was not assigned to a subspecies.

## Evolutionary evidence {#s-dd51d8e554}
Camp Leakey received orangutans that had been reintroduced after displacement. A mitochondrial study found that, among 11 profiles representing founder lineages, nine clustered with the local P. p. wurmbii lineage, while two females clustered with P. p. pygmaeus from northern West Kalimantan or Sarawak; both reproduced with local males. ## Evidence limits {#s-b25e04153b}
These findings come from different places, samples and methods. The Gunung Palung paternity results describe one wild Pongo pygmaeus wurmbii population; the Danau Sentarum nest totals are model estimates for a surveyed landscape; Camp Leakey DNA traces human-mediated reintroduction at one site; and the Usun Apau record is a single sighting with no subspecies assignment. None establishes a current range-wide population estimate, trend or conservation status. The male-form description is genus-level, not a diagnosis across Pongo pygmaeus.
