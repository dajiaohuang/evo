## Home ranges after release at two sites {#s-4805d2f53b}
Researchers released 18 captive-bred axolotls at two aquatic sites in southern Mexico City and tracked them with very-high-frequency (VHF) radio telemetry. The estimated home range, drawn as the minimum convex polygon (MCP) enclosing an animal's recorded locations, averaged 2,747 m² for eight animals at the artificial La Cantera Oriente wetland and 382 m² for ten animals at a restored chinampa wetland field in Lake Xochimilco. The difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001).

The two groups were studied in different seasons: the La Cantera Oriente animals were tracked from 2017-10-27 to 2017-12-07, and the ten chinampa animals were released on 2018-03-12 and monitored through 2018-04-20.

All 18 animals were born and raised in captivity before release. These short, site-specific observations do not show how wild-born axolotls use habitat, whether a population became established, or whether it can sustain itself.

## Pax-family genes in one genome study {#s-37f1b8df39}
The assembled axolotl genome analyzed in a 2018 study lacked Pax3. The study also found that mutating Pax7, a related copy in the same gene family, produced a phenotype similar to that of mice with Pax3 or Pax7 mutations. The genome came from one three-year-old, laboratory-bred d/d adult male.

This genome and its gene-editing experiments do not establish how common the reported traits are in wild populations.

## Microbiome samples from outdoor mesocosms {#s-7fe77f1c90}
Of 22 newly generated Ambystoma mexicanum samples in a 2023 analysis, all came from outdoor experimental systems designed to simulate natural conditions (mesocosms) in the species' former native range at Xochimilco Lake. The systems experienced local climate and used lake water, so the authors retained the samples in all analyses, including mixed models and Mantel tests (tests of relationships between distance matrices). Managed mesocosm animals are not independent observations of wild populations, and the samples do not establish a present natural distribution, native breeding success or microbiome-mediated protection from disease. The 22 samples are not a census of unique animals.

## Climate grids are not animal measurements {#s-cc16afc368}
The study treated sampling habitat as the place and time of collection, separate from the species' life history. Its climate variables came from WorldClim grids: long-term averages covered 1970–2000 and monthly series covered 1960–2018. For 193 pooled samples collected in 2019–2021, the authors used the latest available monthly values, from 2018. That substitution applies to a dataset subset, not necessarily every axolotl sample. Gridded climate and lake water in mesocosms do not measure an animal's body temperature, aquatic thermal limits, water-quality thresholds or present habitat conditions.

## Scope of the evidence {#s-17b5a6d474}
These records come from different settings: one laboratory genome, captive-bred animals tracked after release, historical trait compilations and outdoor mesocosms. Together they do not form a complete account of wild axolotls across their range.
