## A small evergreen with narrow leaves {#s-834c086d23}
Van Wyk’s 1979 account from southern Natal and Transkei describes Eugenia verdoorniae as an evergreen, much-branched shrub or small tree, 1–3 m tall. It may branch from the base or have a single trunk up to 200 mm across. Young branches are reddish brown to brown, then grey with age; they carry sparse, inconspicuous hairs pressed against the surface, and the branchlets become hairless as they mature. Internodes are usually 6–12 mm long, with recorded extremes of 4–20 mm. Leaves are opposite in pairs, with successive pairs set at right angles (decussate); their stalks are 1–2 mm long. Young blades are dark red or pinkish, becoming dark green and glossy above and paler and dull below. The narrow elliptic to narrow linear-lanceolate blades are usually 20–40 mm long (range 15–45 mm) and 5–6 mm wide (range 4–8 mm); their tips are acute or blunt, and the blade narrows from about its middle toward the short stalk. The surface has gland dots and initially bears fine, pressed hairs, especially over the basal third; these hairs disappear with age. The blade is leathery and flat, with an entire edge that turns slightly under only in dried leaves. Veins form a pinnate net. In fresh leaves the midrib is flat on both sides; in dried leaves it is flat or slightly hollowed above along the basal third and strongly raised below. There are 4–6 spreading pairs of side veins, alternate or opposite. They are obscure on both sides in fresh leaves; in dried leaves they are obscure above and raised below, joining into a slightly lobed marginal vein about 0.5 mm inside the edge. Third-order veins are obscure. The petiole is 1–2 mm long and slightly grooved underneath.

## Short racemes, two kinds of flowers {#s-b9b31075b8}
The inflorescences are usually short, axillary racemes with few flowers and no leaves, but they often develop into leafy shoots. A raceme may bear only one or two flowers, and solitary flowers are rare; the flowers have sparse hairs, and the bracts often fall away. Each flower has two bracteoles attached at the base of the flower cup (hypanthium); they are acute and upright, 0.5–1 mm long and 0.3–0.4 mm wide, with sparse hairs mainly along their edges. The account calls the species androdioecious or andromonoecious: male flowers may occur on different plants from bisexual flowers, or both kinds may occur on one plant. It does not state which arrangement applies to the material described. Both flower types have four nearly round sepals, two larger and two smaller. The larger sepals are 1–1.5 mm long by 1.8–2.5 mm wide; the smaller are 0.5–1.3 mm long by 1.5–2.0 mm wide. They are hairless except for a few hairs at the edge, with a few gland dots on the underside. Each flower has four separate, more or less oval petals, about 3–4 mm long and 2.5–4 mm wide; they are white, sometimes tinged pink, not fringed with hairs, and sometimes have about five scattered glands.

## Male and bisexual flowers differ at the ovary {#s-88335945dd}
Male (staminate) flowers are nearly stalkless or have 3–5 mm stalks. Their flower disc is flat with a central hollow, wrinkled, fleshy, and sparsely hairy. It bears 14–22 stamens, usually 16–20; their filaments vary in length, usually 4–5 mm and within a recorded range of 3–6 mm, and are free to the base. Each anther has two pollen sacs, each opening by a lengthwise slit. The anthers can pivot on their attachment, are about 1 mm long by 0.6–0.8 mm wide, and are all fertile. The male flower cup is smooth, inverted-cone-shaped, and about 1 mm long. Its ovary is abortive, its style is a rudiment about 0.5 mm long, and it has no stigma. Bisexual flowers are also nearly stalkless or have 3–5 mm stalks, with the same bracteoles. Their disc is flat with an even surface, fleshy, and sparsely hairy. They have 11–20 stamens, usually 14–16, resembling those of the male flowers. Their inverted-cone-shaped flower cup is 1.5–2 mm long. The ovary is joined to the lower part of the cup and has 2–3 chambers; each chamber has 1–4 ovules, of which one or two develop. The style is threadlike, round in cross-section, smooth, and 4–5 mm long. The small stigma is slightly disc-shaped and covered with tiny papillae.

## A thick-coated seed and a six-part diagnosis {#s-d502043ab8}
The fruit is a berry known only from dried specimens; the account marks its red colour with a question mark. It is obovoid to almost spherical, about 18 mm across, becoming nearly hairless, with the calyx lobes retained at its tip. The fruit wall (pericarp) closely adheres to, but is not fused with, the seed coat (testa). The seed is spherical and smooth, about 10–15 mm across. Its seed coat is woody, tough, fibrous, light brown, and about 0.5 mm thick. The embryo has partly fused cotyledons that are light green and fleshy, without conspicuous gland dots. To distinguish this species from other southern African Eugenia, the account gives a six-part combination: narrow flat leaves whose edges are not rolled under; a midrib flat on both sides of fresh material; in dried leaves, a midrib slightly hollowed above and raised below; spherical seeds with a thick, tough coat; an embryo without conspicuous gland dots; and stems whose first-formed periderm (protective outer tissue) originates in the primary external phloem. This is the cited account’s regional diagnostic combination; it does not quantify how often the characters vary.

## Forest edges, river banks, and a flowering season {#s-c279f7149b}
The habitat note says the plant usually grows at the margins of natural forests or in open sites on the banks and islands of some larger rivers. The account says it flowers throughout the year, mainly from June to July. The cited treatment is regional and gives no dated sampling frame or population frequencies, so these notes do not define the species’ full range.
