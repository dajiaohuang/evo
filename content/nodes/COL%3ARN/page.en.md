## Body regions and silk {#s-148d5cea9a}
Spiders are arachnids, not insects. A narrow pedicel joins the cephalothorax and abdomen; the four pairs of walking legs, pedipalps and spinnerets have different roles. Silk is not just for making webs: the linked account also describes it for capturing or wrapping prey, wrapping eggs, lining retreats and making a safety line.

## How spiderlings disperse {#s-0b7d273406}
The Australian Museum account also describes females wrapping eggs in silk. Spiderlings may disperse by walking, bridging to nearby foliage on a line, or letting fine silk catch the breeze. These are different strategies reported across spiders, not behaviors attributed to every species.

## Source and scope {#s-3179e87402}
This page uses the Australian Museum's introductory account for spider body regions, silk uses and spiderling dispersal. It is not a global taxonomic or complete life-history catalogue, and the behaviors listed here remain limited to that overview.

## Research overview {#s-8ee50091f9}
Three kinds of evidence shape this account of spiders: a review of the fossil record, a Carboniferous arachnid comparison, and a broad target-gene study of living species. Each addresses a different part of spider history, so together they remain a sampled picture rather than one complete, final tree.

## What this evidence can establish {#s-5a71f6db73}
Wheeler et al. infer relationships among the living spiders included in their gene sample. Garwood et al. and the fossil-origin review help define what the early fossil record can support; none of these sources supplies a complete ecological account of the order.

## Spinnerets, fossils and sampled relationships {#s-dc0dd3567e}
Abdominal spinnerets control how spiders deploy silk.

The Carboniferous arachnid *Idmonarachne* provides a comparison relevant to spider origins, but its preserved underside lacks the spinnerets that distinguish true spiders. Its spider-like appearance alone does not place it within Araneae.

Wheeler et al.'s extensive target-gene sampling supports relationships among the living spiders represented in their study. Selden and Penney's fossil review addresses a different part of the record; with Garwood et al.'s *Idmonarachne* comparison, these sources give a sampled, revisable picture rather than a complete, final tree of Araneae.
