## Coat colour and body-size variation {#s-77c274a8b0}
Red foxes are not always bright red: the ADW account describes upper fur from pale yellow-red to deep reddish brown, a white or gray underside, often-black lower legs and either a white or black tail tip. Body size also varies among populations and with latitude. The page’s older length and mass ranges are not reused here.

## Habitats and introduced populations {#s-f7f33285bd}
This fox spans a broad Northern Hemisphere range and has introduced populations in places such as Australia; the source records it in forests, tundra, deserts, farmland and cities. Native range and introductions must be kept separate: an occurrence does not by itself establish native status.

## Breeding and care of young {#s-68234fd90a}
Red fox breeding is not reducible to fixed monogamy: the account records monogamous and polygynous pairings as well as non-breeding female helpers, and reports later breeding seasons farther north. Parents care for young at the den; a male may provision the female while she remains there around birth.

## Diet and food handling {#diet-and-food-handling}
Fox (2007) describes Vulpes vulpes as omnivorous, reports food caching and identifies seed dispersal as a possible ecosystem role. This secondary account does not quantify how often these behaviors occur or establish global interaction networks.

## Native and introduced range labels in a 2007 account {#range-history}
Fox (2007) reports native occurrence across northern North America and the Palearctic, with introduced populations in Australia and the Falkland Islands. The account gives both native and introduced labels to parts of the Neotropics; that ambiguity is retained rather than resolved here. This historical summary is not a current reconciled locality map.

## Six mitochondrial lineages in a Holarctic sample {#mitochondrial-phylogeography}
Kutschera et al. (2013) analyzed 729 mitochondrial control-region sequences and reported one Holarctic lineage, three Nearctic lineages and two lineages restricted to Japan. Their model-based timeline placed several inferred lineage events in the Middle or Late Pleistocene. The sampling map describes study coverage, not a complete current range; these single-locus results are not a genome-wide species tree.

## Two local fossil records and their limits {#fossil-records}
At EVT3 in the Vallparadís Section of northeastern Iberia, dentognathic material dated to about 0.6 Ma was attributed to extant V. vulpes by comparison with V. alopecoides. The authors call it the earliest well-dated European occurrence while noting sparse material and taxonomic uncertainty. Separately, two dentaries (GRCA 76272) from Stanton’s Cave were re-identified as red fox rather than gray fox; the authors call this the first Grand Canyon record and say it may be Arizona’s first fossil evidence. The cave assemblage spans the Late Pleistocene and early Holocene, but the abstract gives no more precise age for these dentaries.

## Global and regional conservation records {#conservation-records}
The linked global IUCN assessment is the 2021 record by Hoffmann and Sillero-Zubiri; it is identified here by its assessment year, not as a 2026 reassessment. A 2021 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listing identifies the Sierra Nevada distinct population segment of V. v. necator as endangered. That regional subspecies/population listing does not describe the status of all V. vulpes.
