## Long feathers bring arms, legs and tail into the flight question {#s-3a17267807}
Dyke and colleagues’ 2013 study considers Microraptor from China’s Early Cretaceous Jehol Biota, with elongated feathers on the arms, legs and tail forming five potential lifting surfaces. “Four-winged” emphasizes the two pairs of feathered limbs, not five limbs. Model construction draws on M. gui holotype IVPP V13352 and BMNHC PH881, referred to Microraptor by Li and colleagues in 2012; the latter retains its genus-level identification here. The feathered model mainly uses that specimen’s proportions and wing outline, scaled for the tunnel. It is not an exact copy at the original size of the M. gui holotype or a measured flight ability shared by all three fixed-roster species.

## Model feathers and joint postures have explicit sources {#s-f2ba79bbcc}
A composite model body carries wing and leg feathers salvaged from living Mallards and Common Wood Pigeons. These approximate relative feather dimensions, not fossil plumage color. Feasible femoral, tibial and tarsal postures draw on specimens and comparisons with the three-dimensionally preserved pelvis of the relative Hesperonychus; the authors exclude an extreme fully sprawled pose. They compare legs down, limited sprawling, main-wing angle and tail area without directly measuring the complete living joint range. Feather orientation to airflow and body posture remain part of the reconstruction.

## Wind-tunnel measurements and trajectory calculations are separate evidence {#s-d932f3ac00}
The feathered model was tested in a 2.1 × 1.5-m tunnel at airflow speeds of 10, 15 and 20 m/s, with geometric angles of attack swept from 0° to 26° in 2° increments and back. Instruments measured lift, drag and moment; the authors corrected for mounting effects and averaged samples. Aerodynamic coefficients were then used in trajectory calculations assuming a 0.5-kg animal corresponding to the model, a 30-m starting height and an initial horizontal speed of 3 m/s. These are experimental settings and calculation inputs, not fossil weighing, an observed launch height or a measured maximum speed.

## A higher lift-to-drag ratio need not mean a stable glide {#s-7405680e80}
Lift-to-drag ratio compares lift with drag; in steady gliding a higher value corresponds to a shallower descent. The authors also examine pitching moment: a higher ratio alone does not select a passively stable trajectory if posture cannot recover after disturbance. At the selected stable points, the model gives about 4.7:1 with legs down and 2.8:1 with limited sprawling. These depend on the particular model, center of gravity and stable points. High-lift, high-drag conditions can generate support quickly at low speeds and reduce initial height loss in short glides. This is not sustained modern-bird flapping or a measurement of the animal’s neural flight control.

## Flat plates test lifting-surface requirements, not feather absence {#s-fd2ae9e9d5}
A second model uses a balsa body, aluminium flat-plate lifting surfaces and a rod tail, tested in a sprawled configuration. Its size, projected areas and estimated center of gravity differ from the feathered model. Similar lift-to-drag ratios at high aerodynamic forces in the stable region lead the authors to argue that effective gliding need not require a highly derived modern wing section. This concerns lifting surfaces under specified model conditions. It does not negate fossil feathers or verify every claim about symmetric feathers, powered flapping or other dinosaurs’ locomotion.
