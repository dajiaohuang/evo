## Sample and classification {#s-1761120b57}
COL26.8 places *Ardea cinerea* in Ardeidae. In AVONET v7, the record uses the HBW–BirdLife 2020 name and includes 13 measured birds, four with a complete morphological set. The usable sample can differ by trait, so the values below are compilation averages rather than a range for individual herons.

## Beak measurements {#s-b2c4899329}
Mean beak length from the front edge of the nostril to the tip is 95.5 mm. At the same landmark, mean width is 18.7 mm and depth is 25.4 mm. These are separate measurements made at standardized positions, not field-identification cutoffs.

## Wing and lower leg {#s-d253c43209}
Mean unflattened wing length, from the carpal joint to the longest primary feather, is 441 mm; mean tarsus length, the measured lower-leg segment, is 137.9 mm. AVONET reports mean mass of 1,443 g from a separate source. That mass estimate does not share the morphological sample count, so it does not show that each measured bird was weighed.

## Wetland ecology categories {#s-98878acdf1}
AVONET classifies the habitat as wetland, the feeding niche as aquatic predator, and the primary lifestyle as terrestrial. These are broad categories in a cross-species dataset. They describe the source’s dominant classifications, not exclusive habitat use or a complete account of how this heron feeds.

## Movement and historical range {#s-1df77b38e4}
The migration field is coded as sedentary. Historical breeding and resident maps span latitude endpoints from −34.84° to 70.43°. These limits summarize the mapped range; they do not trace non-breeding movements or show present-day occupancy. The map summary and body measurements come from different parts of the compilation.

## A second historical life-history record {#s-f6d4e8b449}
A 2015 life-history database also has a historical record named *Ardea cinerea*. Seven selected fields are supported by cited species-level rows with the same name and family. That match links the rows to this page, but the older taxonomy may carry data across historical species and subspecies; it does not establish identity with the current COL26.8 concept.

## Adult and hatchling mass {#s-c2502aac9e}
The 2015 compilation reports adult body mass of 1,443 g and mass at birth or hatching of 45 g. The adult summary can combine males, females and birds of unspecified sex; the second value belongs to a different life stage. They are not measurements of the same individuals over time and do not give an individual mass range.

## Reproductive and developmental timings {#s-bfb7bd9e76}
The same database summarizes female age at first conception as 730 days, offspring per litter or eggs per clutch as 4.31, time from laying to hatching as 25.5 days, and age at flight capability as 48.5 days. Some source records use leaving the nest for the last field. Values are medians, or the mean of the two middle values when the number of records is even; the fractional clutch summary is not a count from one event. Each time refers to a different milestone, not one uniform breeding cycle.

## What the longevity record means {#s-cfc1057674}
The database’s maximum-longevity field is 37.5 years, the largest value in its collected material under the source method. It is not a typical lifespan, a mean lifespan in the wild, or a survival limit for every individual. Conditions are not consistently identified as wild or captive in this compilation.

## The sources behind the life-history values {#s-e7e41222fb}
Life-history fields draw on multiple references, including Dunning (1992), Szekely, Lislevand and Figuerola (2007), de Magalhaes and Costa (2009), and Ricklefs (2010b). The source archive keeps each field’s cited row and value. A row can point to a paper, book or database; row counts are not animal counts, and repeated citations are not independent replication.

## Food records and feeding positions {#s-068d667172}
EltonTraits 1.0 contains another older same-name, same-family record for *A. cinerea*. Its taxonomy predates COL26.8, so the matching name connects records for comparison without resolving later species splits or synonymy.

## Relative weights in the diet record {#s-2bd4138e9a}
The EltonTraits record assigns relative food weights of 60% to fish, 20% to invertebrates, 10% to mammals and birds, and 10% to reptiles and amphibians. Code A indicates high compiler confidence in the source and diet information. The percentages standardize written descriptions; they are not measured food intake, and a category with no listed weight would not prove that the heron never eats it.

## Feeding at the water surface and on the ground {#s-1da872991a}
The archive marks its foraging-height estimate as species-level: 70% is assigned to the water surface or just below it, and 30% to the ground. These weights describe feeding positions in the source record, not nest sites, all habitat or a full-day time budget. They are not elevation bands and cannot establish flight height or habitat area.

## The source linked to the feeding estimates {#s-a917441aba}
Both the diet and feeding-position entries are linked to volume 1 of *Handbook of the Birds of the World*. The book has not been independently checked here, so the percentages remain EltonTraits’ standardized summaries of literature descriptions rather than new observations.

## Limits of these historical records {#s-402df51f0c}
These are historical compilations, not a current species monograph. AVONET’s measurements come from a small sample with trait-specific counts; its mass is separately sourced, and its breeding/resident latitude limits do not describe current occupancy. The 2015 life-history database reports medians or averages of the two middle values, except for longevity, which is the largest collected record. It may inherit values across historical species and subspecies, and its wild or captive conditions are not consistently known. EltonTraits turns literature descriptions into relative food and foraging weights. The underlying studies were not individually reread here, and historical name matches do not prove equivalence with COL26.8. Current movement, population, conservation and local ecological variation need other sources.
