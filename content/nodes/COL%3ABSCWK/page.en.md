## Species rank and the former subspecies combination {#s-b436b0d654}
COL26.8 and the 2025 ICMBio assessment recognize Cacajao rubicundus at species rank. The older IUCN assessment and the 2022 molecular paper used the subspecies combination C. calvus rubicundus. The ICMBio assessment cites the later five-species treatment of bald uakaris; these changing ranks and names should not be mistaken for separate populations.

## Genetic samples do not constitute a census {#s-19b2608049}
The 2022 systematic study included eight cytochrome-b sequences and six ddRAD samples labelled C. calvus rubicundus under the paper's subspecies taxonomy. Those genetic sample counts are not a population estimate or census.

## Three occurrence areas reported by the official assessment {#s-794c9b0932}
The 2025 ICMBio assessment reports three geographically separated occurrence areas in Amazonas, Brazil: Jacurapá, Auati-Paraná and Jutaí-Solimões. It describes records along the Jacurapá channel and says local interviews corroborated occurrence in the várzea between that channel and the Solimões. It also notes contact with C. calvus in Auati-Paraná. These are assessment-reported records and do not establish a continuous or complete range. The assessment infers likely várzea association partly by comparison with other bald uakaris and says the species has not been the subject of ecological studies.

## Separate publication year from category date {#s-b64c2d6a0c}
The ICMBio SALVE sheet was published in 2025, but its Least Concern category is dated 27 September 2019. It reports no significant threats identified in that assessment and cites a 2.31% habitat-loss calculation for 1990–2020 across the known geographic distribution using MapBiomas 6.0. The sheet says total population size, mature-individual count, group size and population trend are unknown, and calls for ecological and population studies. The category date and reported model result are not a new 2025 population survey.

## Evidence limits {#s-d39d77b3c0}
This guide combines taxonomy, regional assessment and direct Mamirauá observations from 2008. Targeted searches, one feeding observation and local social units do not establish annual diet, species-wide group size, abundance or trend. The assessment’s unknown group size and these observed units have different scopes. Historical names are not automatically equivalent across regions. This is not a complete monograph.

## Interviews guided a targeted search {#s-5af9424757}
After interviews at 21 localities in November 2007, seven reported red uakaris. In March 2008, targeted searches around the Aiupiá channel covered 82.5 km on foot and by canoe. Interview reports guided sampling; they were not seven directly verified populations or a density estimate.

## A direct feeding observation with a narrow scope {#s-a5e0e927c5}
The results describe red uakaris feeding on immature Pouteria sp. seeds: two males, a female with an infant, and two juveniles. This is a local feeding observation, not an annual diet or measured food proportion. Earlier fruit-remain evidence was indirect.

## Observed social units and contact with white uakaris {#s-2da146092b}
Left-bank records included a lone animal and social units of 3–9. The authors used “social unit” because of fission–fusion; these are not stable group sizes. At Matias creek, red and white forms occurred about two metres apart, documenting local contact without proving hybridization.

## An older name does not identify every historical population {#s-92f06a206d}
This page uses the Brazilian Mamirauá red form as geographically bounded evidence. The 2025 Quebrada Blanco paper notes that Peruvian C. ucayalii was also called C. calvus rubicundus. Peruvian records under that older name are not imported into current C. rubicundus.
