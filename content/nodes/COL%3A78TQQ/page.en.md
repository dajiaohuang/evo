## Young retained in the male vocal sac until metamorphosis {#s-01eafd71e3}
The 2013 paper summarized earlier observations of male parental care: developing young are incubated in the vocal sac and released after metamorphosis. It contrasted this with R. rufum, whose larvae are released into water before metamorphosis. This life-history comparison is cited background, rather than a new breeding experiment in the survey.

## Presence evidence from the 2008–2012 surveys {#s-0c84f8d84c}
Surveys across Chile and Argentina from October 2008 to March 2012 found this frog at 36 sites grouped into 26 areas. The authors grouped sites less than 2 km apart as subpopulations, using a working distance rule based on other amphibians. Ten additional areas had a single encounter or reliable outside information and were excluded from abundance analysis because population status was uncertain. These are dated detections and study grouping rules, not independently verified present occupancy or genetic population boundaries.

## Forest mosaics and near-ground measurements {#s-3fe0271ef0}
The study found frogs both in small forest openings and in some sites with at least 90% mature forest cover. Monitored microhabitats combined native forest with short vegetation, moss or woody debris. Daytime measurements during austral spring and summer, taken 1–2 cm above ground, recorded relative humidity above 70% and air temperature below 22 °C. The authors proposed habitat requirements from these observations, but the readings are field conditions rather than experimentally established minimum humidity or maximum temperature limits.

## The study’s decline inference and remaining uncertainty {#s-d09d3f914c}
The authors inferred decline from range contraction, anecdotal abundance changes and local disappearances, including protected sites. They explicitly lacked earlier abundance measurements, so the magnitude of population decline could not be accurately calculated. Their proposed 2013 reassessment to at least Endangered was a recommendation; it is not presented here as a current IUCN listing or proof of a single cause of decline.

## A dated multicriteria conservation priority {#s-a49f6166d3}
The 2024 Chilean assessment assigns Rhinoderma darwinii a printed conservation-priority index of 1.18 and places it in the low category in Table 4. The study reviews 58 selected species using literature, citizen-science records and government plans, rather than newly censusing every population. Its index combines distribution, threat, population trend, taxonomic uniqueness and body-size scores with a protected-area term. The value is a management ranking under this model, not an extinction probability, a current IUCN assessment or a direct measurement of population size. “Non-priority” and “low” do not mean biologically safe or no need for conservation.

## The population and protection scores retain their source limits {#s-b28dcf9869}
Table 3 gives Rhinoderma darwinii a population-trend code of 4 (recent decline) and a land-protection code of 2 (less than half within protected areas), using the definitions in Table 2. These are the authors’ ordinal assignments, not newly verified census trajectories or measured protected population fractions. A zero denotes unavailable evidence, not a stable population or zero risk. Threat code 3 is also a grouped source category and cannot identify a specific official status by itself. AOO and EOO scores classify area criteria rather than supplying independently reconstructed occupancy maps; protection coverage does not establish effective management.
