## Accepted name and placement {#s-9e0634857a}
COL26.8 accepts Dryophytes flaviventris Borzée & Min, 2020 as a species. Its recorded classification places it along Anura (order) → Hylidae (family) → Dryophytes (genus). The name and placement refer to the release issued on 20 August 2026; names in another checklist or fossil database require their own identity evidence.

## The South Korean records reported in 2020 {#s-e5721d21a7}
The account reported populations in Buyeo, Nonsan and Iksan, South Korea, and discussed older populations in Gunsan and Wanju that it considered extirpated. These are dated reports under the newly distinguished species concept, not a verification of present-day occupancy or the cause of loss at every site.

## Rice-paddy vegetation and seasonal observations {#s-b30362591a}
The article described frogs on vegetation at rice-paddy edges outside calling periods, with nearby wintering sites. It associated breeding observations with rice cultivation and a late-April to early-July season. Male calls were produced while holding vegetation. Its suggestions about undescribed tadpoles resembling another species are not transferred as established larval morphology.

## Mitochondrial fragments reassembled from earlier RAD data {#s-b44e03f0e5}
The study reuses earlier RAD sequencing reads for immaculatus, flaviventris and suweonensis to assemble mitochondrial genes for COX1 comparison. The reported maximum suweonensis–flaviventris comparison is 1.9%. The species’ reference role is not new field sampling, a whole nuclear-genome analysis or a universal species threshold. It provides no new population-specific diet, breeding or temperature-tolerance measurement.

## Locality-specific recordings within a broad species concept {#s-4be7a3bcff}
The paper groups Iksan (twelve individuals, 468 notes), Wanju (seven individuals, 485 notes) and Gunsan (seven individuals, 914 notes) as the southern side of Chilgap Mountain, labelled Dryophytes flaviventris in its figures but analysed within D. suweonensis sensu lato. Across five sites it recorded fifty-six individuals and 2,858 notes from evening to midnight. Eumseong recordings date to May–August 2012; the other sites were sampled in May2021 and/or June2022. These are separate recording cohorts, not one simultaneous survey, complete breeding calendars or a population census. Individuals were not captured; this paper does not supply new genetic verification of each caller's correspondence to the fixed accepted concept.

## Population means describe notes rather than species limits {#s-f1d523f663}
Iksan, Wanju and Gunsan maximum-power frequencies averaged 3,359.92±9.78, 3,251.38±5.35 and 3,329.86±4.61 Hz, respectively; note durations were 0.14±0.0009, 0.14±0.0006 and 0.16±0.0004 seconds. Table2 identifies the quantities after ± as standard errors, not standard deviations or ranges. The maximum-power frequency is the frequency of greatest note power, distinct from the high-frequency boundary and the 90% bandwidth. Delta time is the duration of a note, not of a whole advertisement-call train. Repeated notes from a caller are not independent individual frogs; these means do not establish species-wide identification thresholds.

## Call variation motivates further integrated taxonomy {#s-1326b7c344}
The study found significant population differences but no consistent north–south pattern matching the proposed mountain barrier. Both sides included notes with two to eight independent pulses. The authors conclude cautiously that their call data support population-level variation and ask for broader genetic and morphological work on flaviventris status. This is not a formal synonymization or proof that the two fixed accepted names represent one species. Table1 PCA retained five variables while excluding pulse count, high frequency and bandwidth, so a rejected variable is not independently certified biologically irrelevant.

## Temperature and roadside noise are separate claims {#s-851f460192}
The recorded temperatures were 16.6–26.1°C; the authors state no significant temperature effect, partly referring to unpublished personal data. They propose traffic noise as an explanation for unusually high frequency boundaries at three roadside sites, but do not experimentally manipulate noise or present a controlled causal test. A high boundary is not the dominant frequency or an audiogram of frog hearing. The sampling conditions do not establish thermal tolerance limits, and the proposed noise mechanism remains untested here.
