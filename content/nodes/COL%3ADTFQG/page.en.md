## sampling window {#s-67d724a017}
Estrada-Grossmann and colleagues studied food boluses regurgitated by cormorants at one wild colony on Peru’s Grande Pescadores Island. They collected a pre-spill sample in November 2018, then sampled from August through November 2022, seven to eleven months after the January oil spill. Because they did not identify or track individual birds, the two samples describe the colony at different times rather than change in the same birds.

## prey composition {#s-bf4f75595c}
Across the samples, the researchers identified five fish species. Peruvian anchoveta (Engraulis ringens) was the predominant prey in both periods. Its numerical share was 99.736% before and 99.168% after the spill; its occurrence share was 98.165% and 99.881%, respectively. The article gives conflicting post-spill totals: Methods and Results report 998 boluses, while Table 1 lists N = 837. The percentages above follow Table 1 as printed; they have not been recalculated.

## interpretation limits {#s-9e82f23e61}
The comparison covers one colony and two periods; it includes no samples collected immediately after the spill. This observational design cannot show that the spill caused a dietary change, or establish that no change occurred. The study did not measure cumulative contaminant exposure. Its findings do not define the species’ diet across its range or describe the population’s current condition.

## catalogue-profile / limitations {#s-b6581a30d8}
The biological findings here come from regurgitated boluses collected at one wild colony on Grande Pescadores Island during two periods. The article’s post-spill total differs between Methods and Results (998 boluses) and Table 1 (837); the table’s percentages are retained as printed. The observational comparison cannot establish an oil-spill effect, an immediate response, cumulative contaminant exposure, or a diet for the species as a whole. The Chinese common name is an editorial translation.
