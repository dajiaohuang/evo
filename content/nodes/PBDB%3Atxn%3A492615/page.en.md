## A species anchored by a partial skeleton {#s-5eec9b7677}
The 2024 description names Haliskia peterseni from holotype KK F1426, now housed at Kronosaurus Korner in Richmond, Queensland. The partial skeleton preserves the front of the skull, lower jaw, both ceratobranchials of the hyoid apparatus, and parts of the spine, ribs and limbs; the paper lists 43 teeth. Major upper-arm and forearm bones are missing. Its measurements describe this individual, not the full variation of the species. The name honours Kevin Petersen, who recovered and prepared it.

## The marine context of the Toolebuc Formation {#s-49ebc76433}
The fossil was collected in November 2021 at Dig Site 3, northwest of Richmond, from Albian strata of the Toolebuc Formation. Spore and pollen biostratigraphy constrains the horizon; it is not a direct date on the bones. Limestone and oil shale in the Eromanga Basin unit record a marine-transgression setting, and associated remains include bivalves and indeterminate ray-finned fishes. This burial context does not establish lifelong marine activity or the species’ full range.

## Teeth, crests and elongated hyoid bones {#s-7257362f6f}
The diagnosis relies on a tooth pattern: the fourth and fifth pairs are smaller than the third and sixth, while the second and fifth alveolar pairs are smaller than pairs three to four and six to eight. Both the premaxilla and lower jaw carry crests, but crest shape alone does not separate related taxa; crushing and excavation damage affect some bones. The complete left ceratobranchial is 280 mm long and the mandible is 400 mm, a ratio of 0.70. Although the paper calls this a hyoid-to-skull ratio in places, these are the two measurements used here; the ratio is not a direct measure of the soft-tissue tongue.

## How the approximately 4.6 m wingspan was estimated {#s-f9c540c88b}
The authors estimate a wingspan of about 4.60 m by comparing the coracoid, fourth metacarpal and first wing-finger phalanx with mature relatives. The humerus, ulna and radius are absent, and later wing-finger bones are incomplete. This is a comparative reconstruction for the specimen, not a directly measured full span, a maximum individual size or a species-wide range. Comparator choice also affects large spans proposed for other Australian pterosaurs from partial jaws.

## Relationships depend on matrices and unstable taxa {#s-f17d612033}
Two character-matrix analyses score about 30.7% and 27.6% of the specimen’s characters. Both place Haliskia within Anhangueria, but finer relationships differ, including results after unstable taxa are removed. The discussion places it near Ferrodraco either as its sister taxon or in a group also containing Mythunga. Neither result names a direct ancestor. PBDB’s 2 October 2026 path connects the species to Haliskia and Anhangueridae; it is a database classification, separate from pinned COL26.8 concepts.

## Feeding hypotheses derived from anatomy {#s-03a18ec0dd}
The specimen preserves no throat or chest contents that directly reveal food. The authors propose that elongated hyoid bones, curved teeth and palate shape might help restrain slippery prey; limited tooth wear also informs a hypothesis of soft-bodied prey such as cephalopods. These are functional and dietary interpretations, not observed fishing or measured prey proportions. The paper cautions that hyoid form alone cannot determine food preference; proposed niche differences among pterosaurs also need firmer age control and independent evidence.
