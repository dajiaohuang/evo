## Accepted name and placement {#s-1ee8a2187e}
COL26.8 accepts Bolitoglossa chinanteca Rovito, Parra-Olea, Lee & Wake, 2012 as a species. Its recorded classification places it along Caudata (order) → Plethodontidae (family) → Bolitoglossa (genus). The name and placement refer to the release issued on 20 August 2026; names in another checklist or fossil database require their own identity evidence.

## Reading route {#s-055584b14b}
Begin with the accepted record and its parent genus, then use the family and order pages to locate related catalogue entries. This page supplies a taxonomic orientation. Species-specific morphology, ecology, life history, distribution, evolutionary evidence and conservation information still require reviewed biological sources.

## Fourteen types and an explicitly defined length endpoint {#s-bd614aae0f}
Female holotype IBH 24708 was collected south of Valle Nacional on Highway 175, Oaxaca, on 26 October 2010 at 676 m. Thirteen paratypes comprised seven females and six males. The holotype’s snout-to-posterior-vent length was 37.2 mm; its separate snout-to-anterior-vent measurement was 34.2 mm. Those endpoints are not interchangeable. The female measured head width 5.6 mm, forelimb 8.8 mm and hindlimb 8.3 mm, and the type series is not a universal population size interval.

## Pad-like webbed feet and holotype tooth counts {#s-a9a2d0e2d9}
The holotype had strongly webbed hands and feet with poorly individualized digits except near their tips, and adpressed limbs separated by about two costal folds. Its counts were 40 maxillary, six premaxillary and 29 vomerine teeth. The diagnosis combined a robust body, webbed feet and comparative tooth and proportional differences. These are individual and sampled comparative anatomical observations, not invariant counts or an experimentally demonstrated climbing adaptation.

## An unresolved tail-ratio label in the holotype account {#s-f3b5cd1136}
The description calls the tail relatively short and prints SVL/TAL = 0.78. The measurement list instead gives SVL 37.2 mm and tail length 29.2 mm, whose tail/body relation is compatible with a ratio near 0.78 in the opposite direction. The printed ratio label is retained as inconsistent with the listed lengths rather than silently rewritten. The two directly reported lengths remain individual measurements; they do not establish allometric or locomotor performance.

## Live orange-brown and preserved golden-brown descriptions {#s-7836e8c6ef}
The live holotype was described with an almost uniform orange-brown dorsum, darker specks, a darker triangle behind the eyes, a coppery iris and pale-brown ventral surfaces with fine cream mottling. In alcohol its dorsum and tail were dark golden-brown and body sides grey-brown; other preserved specimens varied in greys and browns. These source-specific live and preserved states are separate. No single colour is treated as an invariant diagnostic test, toxicity indication or demonstrated camouflage mechanism.

## Daytime banana axils and night vegetation encounters {#s-27c3e13167}
All specimens with encounter information were collected in banana-plant axils by day or on vegetation by night. The authors inferred an arboreal habit by comparison with other Nanotriton salamanders. These collection substrates do not establish obligatory banana use, a quantified day–night activity budget, an egg site or a species diet. The presumed arboreal interpretation remains attributed rather than promoted to an experimental result.

## Known mountain localities and anticipated intervening occupancy {#s-9b746efd8e}
The 2012 account reported Sierra de Juárez localities along Highway 175 between the type site and Vista Hermosa, approximately 1500 m, and Sierra Mixe near Santiago Zacatepec. It compared the mountain locality areas as about 70 km apart and presumed occurrence between them and possibly farther in Sierra Mixe. That presumption is not a confirmed corridor. The discussion’s roughly 255-km² polygon among three localities is an extent-of-occurrence estimate, not occupied habitat area or a measured population size.

## A mitochondrial sister relationship and model-based distances {#s-9ce5dd8f06}
The maximum-likelihood analysis used mitochondrial 16S and cytochrome-b sequences and placed chinanteca as sister to B. occidentalis with bootstrap support 99. GTR distances from sympatric B. rufescens were 0.08 for 16S and 0.21 for cytochrome b, whereas the two chinanteca samples from the type locality and Sierra Mixe had 16S GTR distance 0.004. These model-based distances are not uncorrected p-distances or universal species thresholds. The sampled maternal gene tree is not an independent nuclear-genome species tree.

## Near Threatened as the authors’ 2012 proposal {#s-d7d0d3bbbf}
The authors explicitly lacked population-size and status information, although the type locality yielded individuals on two recent visits. They discussed how a small estimated range plus demonstrated habitat-driven decline might satisfy a threatened criterion, but did not regard the available evidence as establishing that decline. Banana encounters at the forest edge suggested possible disturbance tolerance, leaving forest dependence unresolved. They proposed Near Threatened. This dated interpretation is not an official current listing, a measured declining trend or proof that forest removal is harmless.

## Two mitochondrial markers and one nuclear locus define this study {#s-ccd91772cb}
The 2012 study combined fieldwork from 2005–2011 with museum tissues, reporting ninety-six individuals from fifty-seven localities across its studied Nanotriton sample. Alignments were 522 bases for 16S, 772 for cytochrome b and 481 for nuclear POMC. POMC alleles were computationally phased; two distinct alleles from one animal are not two independent animals. Separate mitochondrial and nuclear gene trees generally supported four named lineages, but neither an individual locus nor this historical sample certifies every contemporary population or catalogue concept. Contact-zone sampling found no cross-species POMC-allele pairs or mitochondrial–POMC assignment discordance; this is no detected hybrid signature, not proof that hybridization is impossible.

## Low sampled 16S divergence and an occidentalis sister relationship {#s-90cd03fc29}
Chinanteca had a reported 16S GTR-model distance of 0.004 substitutions per site between its then-known localities. Both mitochondrial and POMC analyses supported its named lineage and a sister relationship to occidentalis. This is a marker-specific comparison, not a genome-wide diversity estimate, reproductive-isolation test or a universal species threshold. The authors argue that this pair’s divergence likely predates the proposed Pliocene seaway at the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, illustrating that a present geographic boundary need not have caused the initial split. The historical tree does not independently determine a precise divergence date for chinanteca.

## Shared external similarity leaves diagnosis and habitat records bounded {#s-627d47f8f1}
The Background contrasts chinanteca and occidentalis with the other two examined species using maxillary-tooth presence/absence, while warning that the character varies within rufescens. A single tooth character therefore does not resolve every old identification. Chinanteca and rufescens were reported syntopic in Oaxaca, but only a few samples from that locality were available for hybrid testing. The overall collection included banana-plant outer layers, coffee groves, bromeliads and vegetation; it does not assign every substrate or the pooled elevation statement to chinanteca individually. No species-specific diet, clutch or activity schedule was measured by this genetic comparison.

## One sequenced individual and a separate DNA-content estimate {#s-4be32a2d9f}
The study extracted liver DNA from one Bolitoglossa chinanteca specimen as part of a sixteen-species, three-genus comparison, targeting ten gigabases of short-read data per species. Repeat analysis randomly sampled merged reads at roughly 0.1-fold coverage. Table 1 gives a genome C-value estimate of 43.7 picograms, based on blood-smear densitometry and species means from earlier genome-size work. Picograms of DNA are not gigabases, body mass or a complete genome assembly; the sequenced individual and the species-mean DNA-content estimate have different evidence roles. The sample does not determine variation among sexes, populations or ages.

## Detected repeat abundance is a method-dependent lower estimate {#s-ffd866669f}
Table 1 reports 60.2% detectable repeat abundance for Bolitoglossa chinanteca, including uncategorized repeats. Table 2 reports LTRs at 19.17%, and Table 3 separates ERV at 9.42% from Ty3/Gypsy at 9.749%. These are percentages of sampled bases identified by the study's repeat library, not percentages of animals, newly inserted elements or measured gene expression. Mutation can degrade repeats beyond recognition; the authors regard short-read masking as likely underestimating true repetitive content. Rounded table categories need not sum exactly, and uncategorized repeats should not be silently assigned to a known TE superfamily.

## Repeat divergence suggests relative expansion histories {#s-cda6a18471}
The study places this species among four Bolitoglossa with ERV peaks at Kimura distances of 11–13, lower than the main peaks in the six larger-genome Bolitoglossa. Their Gypsy/Ty3 profiles have lower, broader peaks extending to smaller distances; rufescens has a reported additional low-distance peak. These are relative sequence-divergence histories, not absolute dates or direct observed insertion rates. The study could not distinguish element deletion from more effective silencing.

## A local cloud-forest occurrence and its elevation {#s-2b53557bef}
The 2021 Santa Cruz Tepetotutla checklist reports Bolitoglossa chinanteca at 1000–1500 m above sea level during its 2018–2019 survey. Table 1 compares this with an earlier reported elevation of 1500 (Rovito et al. 2012) and lists photographic catalogue number IBH-RF 594. The locality is in the Chinantla region of Oaxaca, Mexico. This is a reported local occurrence, not a surveyed global range, independent verification of the archived photograph, or evidence of occupancy at every intermediate elevation. Historical elevations are retained as attributed comparisons rather than merged automatically with the new observation.

## Interpret the record within the survey design {#s-3130eb1688}
Four observers surveyed pristine and managed cloud-forest sites on eleven five-day trips in 2018–2019, in wet and dry seasons. Three daily windows were 09:00–13:00, 15:00–19:00 and 21:00–01:00; the paper reports 2,640 effective field hours for the whole herpetofaunal inventory. Animals were photographed and released, and GPS locations had a reported 5 m error range. The checklist's Bolitoglossa chinanteca row establishes presence in that sampling context. Total effort and the nineteen amphibian species are not species-specific detection probabilities, population estimates or trends. The accumulation curve had not reached an asymptote, so a missing record cannot establish local absence.

## Dated conservation labels in the checklist {#s-f322f331c3}
Table 1 prints IUCN category NT, EVS entry H (18), Mexican NOM-059 entry Not Included and endemism label Oaxaca SMO for Bolitoglossa chinanteca. These are the source's historical labels, not a newly checked current assessment or legal classification. The IUCN and Mexican columns use different frameworks; a “Not included” entry is not evidence of low risk or unrestricted activity. EVS is retained as the printed index entry rather than a measured extinction probability. The study argues that community-conserved forest supports threatened species, but its presence inventory does not isolate the causal effect of management or demonstrate a population increase.

## Seven fern-perching salamanders {#s-a5a85c973f}
The treatment reports seven individuals on fern leaves adjacent to streams, in both primary montane cloud forest and disturbed areas surrounded by coffee and banana plantations. The locality is1506m, WGS84 latitude17.7251°N and longitude printed as -96.5590°W,15km northwest of the nearest cited San Pedro Yolox locality. Table1 reports this study’s elevation as1000–1500m, which does not include the treatment’s1506m. The authors say the population seems relatively abundant; seven encounters alone do not estimate density, long-term trend or tolerance thresholds.
