## Accepted name and placement {#s-0e2b9c6bd4}
COL26.8 accepts Brachycephalus rotenbergae Nunes, Guimarães, Moura, Pedrozo, Moroti, Castro, Stuginski & Muscat, 2021 as a species. Its recorded classification places it along Anura (order) → Brachycephalidae (family) → Brachycephalus (genus). The name and placement refer to the release issued on 20 August 2026; names in another checklist or fossil database require their own identity evidence.

## Orange adults and fluorescence under ultraviolet excitation {#s-68a8cdfa75}
The 2021 diagnosis reported adult male snout–vent lengths of 13.46–15.92 mm and female lengths of 16.04–17.69 mm, without sample counts in that paragraph. Its combination of traits included an orange, bufoniform body and spotted skull and postcranial plates forming a trapezoidal bony shield, while explicitly reporting no osteoderms. The live-colour account described black eyes and fluorescence seen using a 395-nm ultraviolet flashlight. These are the reported measurements and observations under excitation; they do not establish population size limits, visibility to predators, a signalling function or toxicity.

## Pulsed calls with notes and males kept as different denominators {#s-c783ffe18b}
The bioacoustic methods state that five males were recorded. The results described five call series containing 408 notes, interpreted as advertisement calls, with a note equivalent to one call. The longest series exceeded 265 notes but its beginning was not recorded. Detailed results give note durations of 0.13–0.24 s from 204 measured notes, intervals of 0.11–0.22 s from 198 intervals, 8–13 pulses per note and dominant frequencies of 2.84–4.52 kHz. Notes, intervals, series and frogs are different sampling units. The diagnosis instead prints a duration interval of “0.13–014 s”; that malformed endpoint is retained as a source conflict and is not silently corrected or substituted for the detailed result.

## Two forest sites with different litter and perch settings {#s-6568a424b1}
At Projeto Dacnis, 842 m, the account described a small slow stream, wet or swampy ground and neighbouring slopes with bushes, roots, branches and variable canopy. Frogs were commonly found on slopes and low vegetation, and during drier conditions more often beneath litter. Reserva das Araucárias, 1299 m, was described as flatter forest with dense canopy, deep litter, muddy soil, moss, rocks and fallen wood. At both sites most observed perches were at or below 30 cm; one male perched at 140 cm, and both sexes perched. These observations describe sampled microhabitats, not a complete elevational range, a required perch height or a measured causal effect of vegetation.

## Rainfall association across 370 observations {#s-687c780932}
The activity account used 370 observations and reported a positive association between monthly abundance observations and rainfall, R² = 0.25 and p = 0.005. Frogs were found through the year except May–July in that study, with a September–February peak; one rainy September 2019 survey found 38 specimens. Persistent drizzle could coincide with activity despite low monthly precipitation, so the discussion of months below 200 mm is not treated as a hard physiological threshold. Soil humidity was not measured. Activity below 16 °C during drizzle likewise records field conditions, not a laboratory critical thermal endpoint. Observation counts do not certify 370 unique individuals or population size.

## Daytime calling and limited night observations {#s-9c42dee9b3}
Seven of the 370 observations occurred at night, when the frogs were inactive beneath litter and no calls were heard. The remaining observations were in daylight, including late afternoon; calls and amplexus were observed in the morning and afternoon. The authors did not measure an activity profile for individual daytime hours. This evidence supports the reported predominance of daytime activity at the surveyed sites; it does not prove absence of every nocturnal behaviour, identify a complete breeding season or quantify reproductive success.

## Gaping observations and clay models do not establish predation {#s-a991e11272}
Three handled specimens gaped, and the account also described escape responses. In a clay-model trial, a slaty-breasted wood-rail, Aramides saracura, grasped and moved a yellow frog model. The authors could not be sure that the bird recognized it as Brachycephalus, and they observed no predation on actual frogs. Suggestions that orange colours could resemble forest objects or that yellow failed to warn this bird remain interpretations. Handling responses and model interactions do not establish a natural predator list, toxicity or experimentally demonstrated camouflage.

## A dated wild-boar concern requiring direct evidence {#s-ec9e9d0074}
The 2021 paper reported increasing invasive wild boar, Sus scrofa, in São Francisco Xavier and proposed that their foraging through soil and litter could damage the frog’s microhabitat. It explicitly acknowledged that scientific evidence for this effect was unavailable and called for further studies. The statement is retained as a dated concern and research hypothesis, not a demonstrated decline in B. rotenbergae, a quantified present-day boar trend or an official conservation assessment.
