## Separate the samples in the two analyses {#s-dd1b1a4c19}
The 2021 study ran a first morphological matrix with 64 taxonomic units and 150 discrete characters, using GP/2E 9266 to add body anatomy for T. navigans. Before analysis, the authors coded holotype SMNK PAL 2344 separately and reported no conflicting scores for characters shared with the new specimen; they then used the new specimen’s coding to place the species. A second matrix added MN 6588-V as its own unit; the supplement header lists 65 units and 150 characters. These are comparative tree terminals, not a count of T. navigans fossils or Tupandactylus species.

## Multiple shortest trees and unresolved branching {#s-65f159bfca}
The first search returned 132 most-parsimonious trees, each 371 steps long; with MN 6588-V included, it returned 469 trees of 373 steps. In the strict consensus, T. navigans sits in an unresolved Tapejarinae polytomy with T. imperator, Tapejara wellnhoferi, Europejara olcadesorum and Caiuajara dobruskii. The second analysis adds MN 6588-V to that same unresolved grouping. Tree count and length describe this matrix and search; they are not species counts or probabilities, and they do not show that the two Tupandactylus species are closest sisters.

## Missing scores do not establish shared anatomy {#s-338fd583c1}
The supplements code T. imperator and T. navigans separately, then add MN 6588-V in the second matrix. Many character cells are missing, especially for T. imperator’s postcranial skeleton. The paper notes no differences in the scores available for the two species; missing anatomy means this cannot establish identical whole bodies or prove they are different sexes of one species. Some characters were ordered, and the result depends on the sampled taxa, character definitions, scorable remains and search settings. This guide has not recoded the full matrix or rerun the search.

## Scans and digital models provide different observations {#s-73beac85a8}
Medical CT was used to see bones still enclosed in rock; the reported voxel size is 0.976 mm, with 0.33 mm overlap. The authors segmented and resampled the scans into meshes, then smoothed those meshes for display. A digital model cannot show detail the scan did not resolve, restore missing bones, reverse compression or establish a complete living pose. This account follows the published parameters and reconstructions; it does not add observations from raw CT slices.

## Fused shoulder elements require distinguishing fractures {#s-61f75bfb6b}
Both scapulocoracoids are present, although the left is better preserved and no scapula–coracoid suture is visible. The authors distinguish a transverse fracture near the left coracoid’s proximal end from that joint suture; the right scapula retains an articular surface for the notarium. These bones document the pectoral support system. A fracture is not evidence of an unfused joint, and fusion alone does not give an exact age or launch performance.

## Different humeral preservation does not measure muscle force {#s-a916e14f87}
The left humerus is flattened and partly missing at its distal end. The right is complete, but its proximal and deltopectoral regions remain partly buried and were assessed by CT. Broad muscle-attachment crests and pneumatic openings are described. The humerus-to-femur ratio, about 0.78, belongs to this specimen; crest size is not preserved muscle volume or a direct measure of flight power.

## Wrist and metacarpal anatomy separates small fingers from the wing {#s-2d2e394389}
The wrist preserves proximal and distal syncarpals and the pteroid. Metacarpals I–III are slender and stop short of the wrist, reaching only about halfway along metacarpal IV, which supports the wing finger; the right pteroid is longer than half the ulna. The two hands are exposed differently, and CT was needed for parts of the right side. These bones clarify wing construction, but no complete membrane is preserved and they do not prove a particular climbing or flight behavior.

## The pelvis combines maturity indicators with incomplete development {#s-ddd584da0c}
The pelvis is nearly complete, but the prepubis and small portions are absent, and its sutures cannot be made out. Lateral compression may have rotated the long preacetabular ilium, so the fossil’s orientation need not match the living posture. The authors also describe the ischiopubic plate as apparently incompletely developed. This sits alongside maturity inferred from other fused bones; invisible sutures alone cannot establish reproductive age, sex or a fusion sequence for the whole clade.

## Long hindlimbs and a four-digit foot do not record running speed {#s-9096e005dc}
The tibiae are about one and a half times the femoral length, while the fibulae are shorter. Four toes preserve phalanges; their claws are shorter than the hand claws, and the right foot is incomplete at its tip. These proportions inform hypotheses about support and movement, not measured speed or diet. Neither the fibula-to-tibia ratio nor the toe formula resolves wider morphological disputes.

## The 2.7 m wingspan is a specimen estimate {#s-8ae1d3a39e}
The authors estimate a wingspan of about 2.7 m for GP/2E 9266 from the humerus, ulna, metacarpal IV and wing phalanges. Their tables also report side-specific measurements from bones with different preservation, rather than combining them into one ideal intact individual. The side reported for wing phalanx IV differs between Table 4 and the preservation account and still needs checking against the images or specimen. The estimate is not a maximum for the species or a direct test of flight performance.

## Compare an associated body with isolated cranial material {#s-bf3674f449}
The linked Huaxiapterus corollatus page compares metacarpals and the terminal wing phalanges; the Europejara olcadesorum page estimates span from a skull rather than an associated skeleton. GP/2E 9266 therefore contributes limb proportions, while the isolated head requires relative scaling. Neither comparison records flight itself or establishes performance.

## One skeleton connects the crest with the body {#s-69579ced58}
Beccari and colleagues’ 2021 description centers on GP/2E 9266, a largely articulated Tupandactylus navigans skeleton with skull, postcranial bones and soft-tissue traces. It is referred material, not the holotype SMNK PAL 2344. At publication, it was the first postcranial specimen unambiguously assigned to the genus, linking body anatomy to the skull-based record. That advance does not mean all referred specimens have since been rechecked.

## Six slabs and uneven preservation {#s-350d4bcd48}
The fossil is distributed across six matching limestone slabs, and some saw cuts cross bones and crest tissue. Before accession, thin metal bars had been used to reconnect some broken slabs. Preservation varies: compression, weathering and missing parts include the left fourth wing phalanx and the distal right foot. “Nearly complete” describes the amount of associated skeleton, not uniformly intact preservation. The matrix places it in the Crato Formation, but the exact locality and horizon are unknown; the database’s Nova Olinda collection is not proven to be this specimen’s precise site.

## Bone and soft tissue together form the crest {#s-c99882c8ca}
A slender, almost vertical supra-premaxillary process supports the front of the crest; behind it, the parietal crest is short and rounded. Those shapes help identify the specimen as T. navigans. Preserved crest tissue has a fibrous ventral zone and smoother dorsal zone rising well above the skull, though its uppermost edge is not exposed. CT also shows an anterior expansion of the bony crest beneath matrix. This describes the support and tissue geometry; fossil color patches may reflect oxidation and cannot establish living colors or sex.

## Toothless jaws and a preserved beak sheath {#s-55c3c7c415}
The jaws lack teeth, and the snout turns downward. Tissue sheaths cover the front of the rostrum and lower jaw, continuing over the deep, blade-shaped dentary crest; the authors interpret them as a keratinous beak covering. A gap between upper and lower beak constrains how fully the mouth could close. Handling small food or harder plant material is a functional proposal, not a demonstrated menu: no stomach contents or direct feeding observations are reported.

## Neck anatomy, fused dorsals and individual maturity {#s-6b2a6024e7}
Nine cervical vertebrae are preserved, including a fused atlas–axis complex. The middle neck vertebrae are elongated, the posterior ones shorter, and some postzygapophyses carry lateral longitudinal grooves. The centra of the first five dorsal vertebrae form a notarium, although their neural spines are fused to different degrees. Together with fusion of the wing-extensor tendon process, this led the authors to infer maturity; the unfused tibiotarsus points the other way. Tapejarid growth is not documented well enough to convert this mixed signal into an exact age or species-wide growth curve.

## Clawed fingers beside the elongated wing finger {#s-24f69cebcb}
The first three digits of the left hand are complete, with phalangeal formula 2–3–4 and curved claws that retain a keratinous tissue outline. The fourth digit forms the elongated wing finger. Its terminal left phalanx is missing, while the right wing phalanges can be seen. The claws record grasping anatomy but do not alone prove tree climbing or a feeding behavior. CT segmentation reveals bones within the rock; smoothed meshes aid display and cannot replace missing elements.

## Flight and ground foraging need mechanical assessment {#s-b43d6e2159}
The authors connect the notarium, pectoral muscle attachment area and limb proportions to flight and possible quadrupedal launch, and also discuss terrestrial foraging. The long neck and large crest complicate earlier ideas that treated the crest as an aerodynamic device. Short-distance flight is considered, but the paper calls for more biomechanical study. The skeleton does not directly measure speed, range, launch frequency or a settled ground-dwelling lifestyle.

## Species differences and relationships retain uncertainty {#s-5b41c21d27}
In the 2021 matrix, T. navigans falls within Tapejarinae in an unresolved polytomy with T. imperator and other taxa; the analysis does not establish the two species as closest sisters. The authors compare crest direction and dentary-crest form, and raise—but do not test—the possibility of sexual dimorphism. MN 6588-V is assigned only to Tupandactylus sp., not confidently to T. navigans. The catalogue therefore keeps the two roster species separate and does not turn genus-level material into another species occurrence.
