## Why an outgroup changes the coelacanth comparison {#reading-introduction}
Which living branch lies closest to tetrapods? A genome study examined how the choice of comparison outgroup affects the placement of coelacanths and lungfishes. Its relationship tests and a separate molecular-clock estimate answer different questions; neither identifies a fossil as a direct ancestor.

## A closer outgroup was not always the better one {#s-1cf115f334}
A phylogenetic outgroup is a lineage outside the group being tested, used to orient comparisons. Takezaki and Nishihara compared three data sets for living coelacanth, lungfish and tetrapod relationships; their newly assembled matrix contained 831 genes and 242,475 ungapped amino-acid positions.

With cartilaginous fishes as the outgroup, the analyses strongly supported lungfishes and tetrapods as sister lineages. Using ray-finned fishes instead shifted the strongest support to a coelacanth–tetrapod pairing, but that support was weaker. The authors reported that sampled ray-finned-fish branches were 35–50% longer on average than the cartilaginous-fish branches and had more divergent amino-acid frequencies. This is a warning about sampling and models in a living-lineage tree, not evidence that a coelacanth was an ancestor of tetrapods.

## A calibrated split, not a fossil first {#s-86542e4c32}
A separate study sampled 61 actinopterygian and sarcopterygian species at 21 independent loci and applied 24 fossil calibrations in a relaxed-clock analysis. It estimated the crown Sarcopterygii split between the coelacanth lineage and the lungfish-plus-tetrapod lineage at about 409 Ma. This is a model estimate for divergence among living lineages, not the oldest coelacanth fossil or a complete fossil range for the recorded Coelacanthimorpha topic.
