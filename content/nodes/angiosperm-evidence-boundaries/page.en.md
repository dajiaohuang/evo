## Flowering-plant history through seven evidence windows {#s-4b9df9a103}
Move from clock calibrations to whole-plant fossils, then to regional grass proxies; each record answers a different question.

## A clock estimate depends on its calibrations {#s-dd69a10f79}
A 2026 analysis combines molecular data with 25,685 fossil occurrences to estimate when the crown group of living flowering plants—their last common ancestor and all its descendants—began. Its preferred hard-bound treatment gives a narrow Late Jurassic result, while other calibration choices yield much older estimates. These are clock outputs, not a fossil flower dated to the group’s origin.

## A separate model dates major crown branches {#s-4038b33815}
A separately calibrated time tree places several major branches within the living flowering-plant group near 135–130 Ma. These are modelled divergence estimates; they do not validate older atlas ranges or record the first fossil appearance of each branch.

## What a thousand Montsechia fossils preserve {#s-8d74da5401}
More than one thousand Montsechia specimens preserve leafy axes and closed, single-seeded fruits. Their depositional setting supports interpreting this extinct plant as submerged in freshwater. That local habit does not show that the ancestor of flowering plants was aquatic.

## Leefructus links leaves to reproduction {#s-87f18c72f7}
Leefructus preserves diagnostic leaves together with reproductive organs in a Yixian bed dated to 125.8–122.6 Ma. That association supports an early fossil record for eudicots, a major flowering-plant branch, while its proposed Ranunculaceae affinity remains provisional.

## Cratolirion preserves the whole plant together {#s-82e42e9cfc}
At about 115 Ma, Cratolirion preserves roots, leaves, internal anatomy and reproductive organs in one whole-plant record. A monocot is a flowering plant lineage characterized by one seed leaf; a crown-monocot placement does not make this fossil a modern lily or the first member of that lineage.

## Grass phytoliths show a regional shift {#s-4e9a1a9c70}
Fourteen Great Plains palaeosol assemblages—ancient soils—show an 8–5.5 Ma rise in phytoliths attributed to grasses that may use the C4 photosynthetic pathway. Phytoliths are microscopic silica bodies formed in plant tissues. The reported 50–60% share is within the grass sample, not a measure of all vegetation cover.

## Tooth enamel tracks C4 biomass, not grassland cover {#s-ceb619e6e6}
Carbon isotopes in tooth enamel preserve a dietary signal that tracks widespread but regionally asynchronous C4 biomass expansion around 8–6 Ma. The proxy does not identify the exact C4 plant lineages and does not directly measure how much open grassland covered the landscape.
