## A regional breeding-season sample with distinct analysis subsets {#s-f73a214622}
The study collected 266 Nanorana quadranus in the 2020 and 2021 breeding seasons at 13 sites in mountainous Sichuan, Chongqing, Shaanxi and Gansu, spanning sampled elevations of 586–1,702 m. There were 134 males and 132 females overall, but the testis Methods specify 133 dissected males, sperm-characterization Methods a subset of 56, male analyses ten populations, and female analyses twelve. Those denominators are kept separate. The sampled elevational interval is not the species’ complete range or physiological tolerance, and the field sex counts do not establish an unbiased wild population sex ratio.

## Age estimates come from growth lines rather than known birth dates {#s-56091e6dfe}
All 266 sampled frogs had distinguishable lines of arrested growth in toe-bone sections. The authors describe checks for resorption, double lines and false lines, and cite validation using four marked–recaptured individuals. This supports their age-estimation procedure but is not an independently reconstructed known-age dataset for the current sample. Cross-sectional body-size-versus-age regressions compare frogs of different estimated ages; their slopes are not longitudinal growth trajectories or maximum lifespan estimates.

## The female-biased size contrast is sample-specific {#s-7aafbcb435}
Females had a higher median body mass and longer median snout–vent length than males, with reported p values below 0.001. Their age distributions overlapped with similar medians but females showed greater dispersion. The reported body-length/age slopes were 6.4 for males and 5.25 for females, both significant in the authors’ regression. These differences do not prove fecundity-driven selection, longer female lifespan or a directly measured growth–reproduction trade-off. Individual size ranges and the raw age distributions were not independently checked.

## Relative testis size varies without establishing mating success {#s-5ce02dd8c3}
The authors report differences in relative testis size among ten sampled populations, persisting after age and body-length adjustment, and a positive latitude association (r = 0.350, p < 0.001). Wangcang, Wanyuan and Nanjiang are among the populations described as relatively larger. Population identity in an observational model is not an experimentally established driver, and enlarged testes do not directly measure sperm competition, fertilization success or greater energy allocation. The Discussion’s comparative ranking against other amphibians is cited context, not a new standardized survey of more than 130 species.

## Asymmetry results require both the omnibus and pairwise findings {#s-45f15ecd55}
Paired tests found no significant left–right size difference within the analysed populations, and the omnibus absolute and relative asymmetry tests were nonsignificant (p = 0.195 and 0.180). Nevertheless, LSD comparisons describe greater asymmetry in the 1,518 m population, with 14 frogs, than some other groups. These findings do not establish perfect anatomical symmetry throughout the species. A pairwise exception after nonsignificant overall tests requires caution; the proposed plasticity or developmental-noise explanations were not experimentally distinguished here.

## Sperm dimensions are population means from prepared slides {#s-5c822aee15}
The reported mean head lengths across the sampled populations range from 27.67 to 55.8 μm, mean tails from 51.1 to 102.61 μm, and total mean sizes from 79.57 to 158.42 μm. The Methods use a subset of 56 males, twenty intact sperm per sample measured in triplicate, after drying, fixation and staining. These intervals concern reported population means, not the full individual-cell range or live sperm speed. A positive sperm-length/testis-size association does not establish a competitive advantage, flagellar performance against stream flow or greater fertilization success.

## Adjusted sperm-count results differ from unadjusted comparisons {#s-4b29c765d2}
Sperm counts differed among populations in the unadjusted analysis, but not after adjusting for body size, age and testis size (reported p = 0.170). The latitude trend was weak and nonsignificant; the Discussion’s interpretation that northern populations prioritize quantity is therefore a hypothesis. Nonsignificance of the sperm-count/length association does not prove that an evolutionary trade-off is absent. The article reports differing analytical degrees of freedom, and the original supplementary rows have not been reconciled to certify the count-analysis denominator or the suspension-based total-count calculation.

## Ovarian ova estimates do not equal successfully laid clutches {#s-f22174f86b}
The researchers dissected 132 females from twelve populations, counted mature ova in a weighed portion of an ovary and extrapolated to total reproductive output. The Methods also describe absolute investment as total egg volume, while later sections use fecundity and fertility terminology. These related endpoints are retained without equating count, mass and volume. Population differences became nonsignificant after body size, mass and age adjustment, while female mass was positively associated with output (reported r = 0.664). Neither ova estimates nor body-mass correlations establish eggs laid, fertilization, hatch success or experimentally measured energy reserves.

## Environmental models leave much reproductive variation unexplained {#s-11285c9dbc}
The Results report models explaining 34.7% of relative-testis variation, 31.5% of sperm-size variation and 10% of female output variation. The sperm-size Discussion instead states 35%, and its selected predictor lists also differ from those in the Results. These inconsistencies are not silently resolved without the supplementary models. Climate rasters and site covariates establish associations under the authors’ selection procedure, not genetic adaptation, causal rainfall or temperature effects, or a measured reproductive threshold. Sexual selection, resource allocation, sperm motility and mating success were not directly tested.

## Population-sensitive management is a proposed application {#s-11c25582b4}
The authors recommend protecting habitat mosaics and monitoring reproduction under climate change. Their Near Threatened wording is dated to the 2025 article, not a newly verified official assessment. Smaller versus larger egg strategies and delayed maturation are discussed as interpretations and comparisons; egg-size trade-offs and individual maturation schedules were not resolved by this study. The observations do not certify a climate projection, improved management outcome or future survival of every population.

The total list contains 25 species, with 20 from field surveys and five from literature. It is not a year-round census, a juvenile demographic study or an independently checked voucher identification.

## Regional gradients and survey gains do not certify recovery {#s-c6cf2df65a}
The reserve description spans 1,150–3,830 m, the sampling Methods report 800–2,600 m, and statistical Methods describe observed amphibians at 800–2,500 m. These scopes are retained rather than assigned as this species’ measured range. Richness peaks in two regional bands but its linear elevation association is nonsignificant (reported R² 0.114, p 0.412). Historical recorded-species increase is also nonsignificant (Mann–Kendall p 0.148). Neither result establishes unchanged populations, increased abundance or a causal benefit of management. Discussion and Conclusions use stronger fragmentation, conservation-success and large-population language than these tests alone support; raw records and historical sampling comparability have not been independently reanalysed.
