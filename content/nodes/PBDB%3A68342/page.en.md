## Fourteen earlier skeletons and thirteen new excavations {#s-58f01f4e0a}
Varricchio and colleagues’ 2008 study examines a Sinornithomimus bonebed near Suhongtu in Inner Mongolia, distinguishing 14 previously collected individuals from 13 newly excavated partially articulated skeletons. The new material ranges from a pelvis with some limbs to nearly complete skeletons; Table 1 lists LH PV5–PV17, rather than an additional set beyond those thirteen. One database collection on the occurrence guide is not one animal. The study discusses an aggregation exceeding twenty individuals, but preservation, orientation and histological sampling have separate denominators; not every skeleton is complete or individually aged by histology.

## Limbs sunk into mud and exposed trunks {#s-8edafd67df}
Twelve of the thirteen newly excavated skeletons retain articulated pelves and hindlimbs, many in a life-like crouch. Flexed hindlimbs, ventral pelvic shafts and some forelimbs cut downward through bedding to depths of up to 40 cm; some tail sections extend 25–30 cm below the surface. The authors interpret entrapment in soft mud, noting that compaction can reduce recorded penetration depths. Trunks near the former surface are more often disarticulated or incomplete than buried parts; nine of thirteen lack both ilia. Exposure, decay and possible scavenging help explain loss, without demonstrating long-distance transport of whole carcasses or identifying a scavenger.

## Mudcracks, ostracods and the miring interpretation {#s-162e99f08d}
Most skeletons occur low in a 1.3 m fine-grained unit associated with deformed laminae, sand-filled mudcracks, ostracod accumulations and V-shaped structures interpreted as partially collapsed toe impressions. These sedimentary clues, together with deeply embedded skeletons, support mass miring as a lake or pond dried; the authors regard miring as a more immediate cause than drought. They do not establish an individual sequence of death through dehydration, starvation or predation. Exposure is insufficient to determine the waterbody’s area, and the site does not map the species’ complete habitat or seasonal movements.

## How aligned skeletons support a traveling group {#s-b969f3a185}
Skeleton directions were measured toward the head along a line perpendicular to the line connecting both sides of the pelvis. Ten of thirteen lie between 87° and 188°, with a strong southeast preference and reported probability of chance alignment p < 0.002. Proximity on one bedding plane, similar preservation and aligned bodies jointly support a group arriving and dying together; isolated elongate elements show a different orientation pattern. The significance concerns orientation, not group stability, kinship, leadership or defensive behavior, and does not resolve simultaneous death at every instant.

## Thin sections from three individuals and minimum ages {#s-dc1c2c3f53}
Histology sampled only LH PV5, PV6 and PV7, with femur lengths of 21.6, 19.4 and 36.4 cm respectively, examining tibiae, fibulae and ribs. A single line of arrested growth (LAG) in ribs/fibulae of the two smaller individuals was interpreted as about 1–2 years at death. The corresponding feature in the LH PV5 tibia is a band of laminar bone: Figure 4A explicitly shows no actual LAG, so not every element carries the same line. The larger individual’s tibia/fibula preserve four LAGs, with earlier records possibly removed as the medullary cavity expanded; its rib has seven, supporting discussion of at least seven years. Annual interpretation, remodeling and element differences remain explicit; seven lines do not establish exactly seven years or maximum lifespan.

## An immature group and unproven breeding roles {#s-2e7c8dde22}
The larger newly excavated individual retains open neurocentral sutures, while developing outer osteons support continued rapid growth. The assemblage is interpreted as juvenile to subadult, without hatchlings or mature individuals, but each animal was not individually aged from thin sections and sexual maturity was not measured directly. The paper further proposes that nonbreeding individuals might aggregate while breeding adults nested and cared for young. This is a life-history hypothesis: the bonebed does not directly establish these Sinornithomimus nests, parentage, adult locations or division of care. Its age-skewed sample is not a census of the entire population.

## Preservation meaning of gastroliths and carbonized contents {#s-1db39e8eac}
Intact gastrolith masses inside some ribcages, carbonized stomach contents and sclerotic rings within orbits are used to support limited exposure and relatively rapid preservation; Figure 3D shows a stone mass and carbonized material before and behind it. The article discusses earlier herbivory interpretations but supplies no plant-species inventory or quantified diet from that carbonized material in this study. Gastroliths and stomach contents are different evidence, and good preservation does not identify food in every dark patch. The authors leave the link between dietary change and complex sociality unresolved, rather than demonstrating herbivory as a cause of herding.

## Read stratigraphic age separately from age at death {#s-4c45734ad8}
The paper discusses an approximately 92 Ma maximum age for the Ulansuhai Formation from dating underlying volcanic rocks and uses a Late Cretaceous designation. The occurrence guide retains PBDB’s 93.9–89.8 Ma/Turonian database interval. No dating is recalculated, and the underlying-rock maximum is not a precise death date for the bonebed. Geological time in millions of years and individual ages inferred from growth lines operate at different scales. Juvenile proportions in a catastrophic event inform the group present then, without directly determining ordinary juvenile mortality, a complete survivorship curve or species-wide population structure.
